## 60-second verdict

This page is built from the 2026 official and ITRA material read here, for trail runners weighing UT100 or the 100 miler. **It suits** runners with a long mountain-distance base who can hold rhythm on Table Mountain's steep pitches and technical descents, and who can plan fueling, warmth and a night leg against a 26-hour (UT100) or 45-hour (100 miler) limit. **It does not suit** first-time 100 km runners, runners without night-race experience, or those unprepared for Peninsula daytime heat, exposed ridge wind and technical descents. Three decisions come first: pick the distance — UT100 is the long-standing main distance, the 100 miler is the 2026 flagship with a qualifier; check eligibility — the 100 miler needs a 50 km+ trail finish within 24 months submitted before noon on 28 October; and read distance and gain on the organiser's own terms — this page keeps the official card, ITRA listing and GPX measurement side by side without ruling between them.

- **Who it suits**: advanced trail runners with sub-100 km experience, steep pitches above 35 degrees and technical descents handled, and some night racing behind them.
- **Who it does not suit**: first-time long-distance runners, those without night navigation practice, or those under-prepared for heat exposure and windy ridges.
- **What to do first**: compare qualifier rules and fee bands before choosing a distance; use the official GPX and course description to assess steep climbs, technical descents and the night leg; complete the compulsory gear list and station plan from the official race-info and briefing.

## From 14 finishers in 2014 to today's six races

The official UT100 page uses two medals to tell the event's history: the Nic Bornman Medal goes to the top ten men and women of UT100 in honour of the race founder Nic Bornman, and The Originals medal goes to anyone finishing UT100 under 15 hours, recalling the inaugural 2014 race where only 14 people finished inside the brutal 15-hour cut-off.

The first 2014 edition had only the UT100 main distance and a 15-hour limit. Today the event has six distances: 100 miles, 100 km, 55 km, 35 km, 23 km and 16 km; the 2026 entry page shows every race closed.

The 100 miler is the current flagship: from Cape Town's northern mountains to the southern peninsula via Silvermine, Kalk Bay, Kommetjie and Simon's Town, then back through Constantia and UCT. It adds about 64 km over UT100 and roughly 2,000 m of gain, with the exact difference depending on which gain figure is used; this page does not turn that into a metre-perfect number.

## The 100 km course

The organiser-supplied description on ITRA calls UT100 front-loaded: the first 50 km contains the two significant climbs on Table Mountain and Karbonkelberg, while the second half has a similar amount of gain but in more sustained gradients.

- The race leaves the city, climbs Signal Hill and goes around Lion's Head, with views over the city and the Atlantic seaboard.
- It climbs midway up Table Mountain's north face and contours to Platteklip Gorge. The organiser calls this the longest vertical ascent of the race, with gradients over 35 degrees, and says 1,700 m of gain is covered by 25 km.
- On top of Table Mountain the route crosses the tabletop ridge, then takes a new section along the 12 Apostles before the technical descent of Llandudno Ravine to a beach aid station.
- The route flattens along Sandy Bay, then takes on the 600 m Suther Peak and a traverse of Karbonkelberg to the Hout Bay aid station. The organiser calls this the make-or-break section, especially as temperatures rise.
- From Hout Bay the second half crosses the beach to East Fort and the Manganese Mines, goes over the mountain above Hout Bay, descends via Vlakenberg to Constantia Nek, then crosses vineyards and city greenbelts to the Alphen Trail aid station.
- It then enters the technical Cecilia and Newlands forests, drops steeply below Rhodes Memorial and reaches the last aid station on the edge of the University of Cape Town.
- The final 10 km climbs to the Blockhouse and traverses lower Devil's Peak before the finish.

![Lion's Head from Cape Town's central business district, the peak UT100 goes around after the start (2026; not a race photograph)](/media/routes/utct/lions-head-2026.jpg)

The organiser adds that the 12 Apostles and Llandudno Ravine section is new, that it was requested and only recently permitted because of congestion at Constantia Nek. That says the 100 km route has been adjusted at least once, but it gives no year and no before-and-after description. This page therefore logs it as one `courseChanges` entry, with the added year left unpublished.

## The 100 miler: flagship and pacing

The 100 miler is not UT100 with an extra loop. The official description takes runners from the north to the south of the peninsula: up Kloof Corner and the north face of Table Mountain, through Llandudno and Hout Bay, then out to Silvermine, Kalk Bay, Simon's Town, Scarborough, Kommetjie and Noordhoek, before the final UCT-to-King's-Blockhouse climb and the contour back to Gardens.

![Hout Bay Harbour and the mountains behind it, passed by both UT100 and the 100 miler (2009; not a race photograph)](/media/routes/utct/hout-bay-2009.jpg)

The official card and prose give different gain figures: the card has **163 km / 7,187 m D+ / 45 hours / fourteen aid stations / 17:00 start**, while the opening prose says **7,516 m**. This page keeps both first-party figures side by side; ITRA's +6,995 m race-details table and 7,850 m organiser-supplied description are also listed, with no averaging.

Entry to the 100 miler requires a qualifier: a 50 km or longer trail race completed within the 24 months before race day, emailed to the organiser by midday on 28 October. The page says that without a qualifying result by that deadline, the runner forfeits the right to participate. The page writes "28 October"; the year is read as 2026 for this edition.

Pacing is allowed only in a fixed zone: 105.7 km from the Llandudno aid station to the Alphen Trail aid station, with seven pick-up points. A runner may register up to three pacers but use only one at a time; pacers cannot carry the runner's gear and cannot join or leave at non-designated aid stations.

Finishing the 100 miler under 30 hours earns the Honey Badger medal. The official page says only 43 people had achieved that before, with just five having repeated it; those are the page's cumulative figures, not a single-edition count.

The 2026 100-mile open prize money is R80,000 for first, R40,000 for second and R25,000 for third; UT100 pays R75,000 / R37,500 / R18,750. Both lists cover only the open top three and do not include age-group or team awards.

## Timing and cut-offs

The official UT100 page confirms 26 hours and nine aid stations; the official 100-mile page gives 45 hours and fourteen. Neither page publishes cumulative kilometres for the stations or per-station closing times, so this page does not calculate station gaps or turn the total limit into a pace plan.

The published UT100 description makes the difficulties clear:

- **The legs are spent early.** With 1,700 m of gain by 25 km and Platteklip Gorge steeper than 35 degrees, a fast first half is paid for later.
- **Technical descent and coastal heat.** Llandudno Ravine is a technical descent, and the Suther Peak and Karbonkelberg section is at its hardest as temperatures rise. The organiser calls it the race's dividing line.
- **Two big climbs, both early.** Table Mountain and Karbonkelberg both fall in the first 50 km; the second half has similar total gain but fewer breaks.
- **Unknown station spacing.** Nine official stations over 99.1 km look reasonably distributed, but the official page has not published the kilometres. Until a station table exists, this page does not turn that into a hydration rule. The race is cupless: runners must carry their own cup and the full compulsory kit.

The 100 miler scales the problem up: 45 hours, fourteen aid stations, a longer and more southerly route through Silvermine, Kalk Bay, Kommetjie and Simon's Town, with a fuller night section. The official page publishes no per-station data, so this page does not extrapolate; what is verifiable is its distance, card gain, limit, station count and start-finish.

By card arithmetic: the 100 miler's 17:00 start plus 45 hours gives a 14:00 cut-off on 22 November; UT100's 04:00 start plus 26 hours gives 06:00 on 22 November. Both are derived clock times, not separately published official cut-offs, and neither replaces the race-day briefing or any per-station cut-off.

## Airport and city transport

- **Airport:** Cape Town International (CPT) is about 20 km from the city centre, roughly 20–30 minutes by road; this is a normal city estimate, not a race-day transfer promise.
- **MyCiTi:** the A01 route runs from the airport to the city about every 20–30 minutes and takes about 30 minutes; MyCiTi requires a MyConnect card and does not sell tickets on board for cash.
- **Race day:** city transport only gets you to the city, not to the start; it does not replace the race Park & Ride. Check the official schedule for shuttle times and do not treat A01 airport times as start transfers.

## Weather and environment

Open-Meteo's Cape Town record for 20–22 November, 2015–2025 (33 days): daily maximum 16.5–30.2 °C, median 23.6 °C; daily minimum 9.8–17.4 °C, median 14.8 °C; 10 of 33 days had at least 1 mm precipitation, 5 had at least 5 mm.

This is the Cape Town city grid, not the Table Mountain ridge or exposed Chapman's Peak. November can be 30 °C, or wet, windy and about 10 °C at night; the 17:00 100-mile start crosses a full night cooling, while the 04:00 UT100 start meets mountain wind before sunrise. The organiser publishes no race-period mountain measurements, and this page does not treat city data as ridge data.

## Qualification and entry

ITRA lists registered caps of 350 for the 100 miler, 450 for UT100, 500 for the 55 km, 500 for the 35 km, 750 for the 23 km and 500 for the 16 km. The UT100 and 100-mile pages currently mark ENTRIES AS CLOSED; the other races' open status should be checked on their own pages.

The official entry page, headed with a 26 February 2026 opening, lists six prices.

| Race | 2026 entry fee |
| --- | ---: |
| 100 miles | R6,195 |
| UT100 | R5,195 |
| Peninsula Traverse 55 KM | R3,755 |
| Table Mountain 35 KM | R2,655 |
| Explorer 23 KM | R955 |
| Kickstarter 16 KM | R1,595 |

The same page's cancellation table offers a 60% refund before 1 July, 50% from 2 July to 1 September, 40% from 2 September to 1 October, 30% from 2 October to 31 October and 0% from 1 November. Substitutions run from 1 October to 1 November with a R250 admin fee. No deferrals are accepted except for pregnancy with a doctor's certificate, valid for one year and submitted by 2 November. Downgrades before 3 November get no partial refund; upgrades before 3 November pay the difference if the race is not sold out.

**Edition note:** this entry page is headed 2026 but its embedded terms paragraph still names "17 to 23 November 2025". The price table is treated as the 2026 page; the refund and substitution clauses are recorded as page text, but a runner must confirm them in the 2026 entry email and the formal terms. This page does not present them as a word-for-word verified 2026 contract.

Age limits: 16+ for Kickstarter 16, 18+ for Explorer 23 and 20+ for Table Mountain 35, Peninsula Traverse 55, UT100 and the 100 miler. All races require the full compulsory kit for the entire race; the event is cupless, so runners must carry their own cup.

Elite entry: international elites with an ITRA performance index above 800 for men or above 690 for women can request a free entry; South African elites are handled case by case with reserved and free places. Race categories are Open 18–39, Vets 40–49, Masters 50+ and Grand Masters 60+.

The 100-mile qualifier differs from UT100: a 50 km+ trail finish within 24 months, submitted by midday on 28 October. The UT100 page read here does not list an equivalent compulsory qualifier, so the official entry conditions still govern.

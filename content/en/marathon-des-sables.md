## What this race is

The Marathon des Sables Legendary is a multi-day stage race in the Sahara: six race stages over consecutive days, food self-sufficiency, and a return to the bivouac each night. The 2026 regulations define it as:

> Le MARATHON DES SABLES Legendary 40ème édition est une course à pied, ouverte aux marcheurs, par étapes, en autosuffisance alimentaire et en allure libre sur une distance de 250 kilomètres environ, avec obligation pour chaque concurrent de porter son équipement (nourriture et matériel obligatoires).

The official 2027 event page summarises the format as "approximately 250 km, 6 stages, 11 days including 9 in the desert, food self-sufficiency". But the six official stage distances on the 2026 live page add up to **270.0 km**, not 250 km. Both official figures are kept; this page does not average them or spread the difference across a stage. The card uses the stage sum of 270.0 km because it must reconcile with the `stages[]` data; the article shows both figures side by side.

### What makes it different from a single-day trail race

- **It is not one continuous run.** There is one stage per day and a return to the bivouac; stage 4 spans two days and is followed by a rest day.
- **Food self-sufficiency.** The organiser supplies no food; competitors carry food for the whole race. The organiser guarantees water.
- **No stage-to-stage baggage transfer.** The regulations state that during the race the organisation takes charge of no item in a competitor's backpack. This differs from multi-day races that truck bags to the next camp.
- **The route is secret.** Only the roadbook is issued; no public GPX. Navigation relies on markers, a compass, a GPS beacon and checkpoints.

The 2010 race photograph below illustrates the surface and the self-supported load, not the 2026 course: runners carrying mandatory equipment climb a rocky slope, with the rest of the field strung out along the same face. The Commons file page identifies only the 2010 Marathon des Sables; it gives no stage, kilometre or location, so this page does not use it to infer gradient, length or position.

### How stage 4 and the rest day are represented

The 2026 programme says 8 and 9 April are stage 4 ("La longue"), run over two days, and 10 April is stage 5. The official 2026 live page also labels stage 4 "april 8 and 9, 2026", with a latest arrival of "9:00 PM (D+1)". The official 2027 event programme goes further and labels the second day of stage 4 (8 April 2027) "Repos".

This page therefore uses **9 April 2026 as a rest-day placeholder with distance 0**: for runners who have finished stage 4 it is a rest day; for those still on the stage 4 course it is still race time. The placeholder adds no distance and no cumulative time; the cumulative limit stays at 62 h. The six stage distances still total 270.0 km, and the cumulative sequence still connects.

### How to read the cut-offs

Article 11 of the 2026 regulations says stages 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 have a maximum allowed time of about **10 hours**; Article 12 says stage 4 is the long stage, 65–90 km, partly at night, with a maximum of **32 hours**. Each checkpoint's closing time is printed in the roadbook, and missing it means elimination.

The official 2026 live page gives a second, more precise set of latest arrival times: stage 1 10 h, stage 2 11 h 35 m, stage 5 12 h 5 m, stage 4 38–40 h depending on a 05:00 or 07:00 start. Both sets are official; this page lists them side by side and neither averages nor overrides the other. `stages[].cutoffHours` uses the regulation values (10 / 10 / 10 / 32 / 10 / 10) because the regulation is the rulebook; the table also shows the live-page times.

**The cumulative cut-off is not an official figure.** The organiser publishes no "hours accumulated by stage X" overall limit. The 10 / 20 / 30 / 62 / 62 / 72 / 82 h values are derived by adding the regulation stage limits; they are shown only to explain the structure and should not be treated as an official overall cut-off. The regulations also publish no single overall race time limit.

## Weight and self-sufficiency

This section needs to be read on its own, because "self-sufficiency" at MDS is often mistaken for "nothing is provided".

### Official minimum calories

Article 24 of the 2026 regulations says:

> Entre le 05 et le 10 avril, lors des étapes chronométrées, chaque concurrent devra disposer obligatoirement de 12 000 k/calories minimum soit de 2 000 k/calories par jour de course sous peine de pénalités (voir ART. 27 et 28).

In other words, for the timed stages from 5 to 10 April each competitor must carry at least **12,000 kcal**, counted as **2,000 kcal per race day**. There is no calorie minimum on the final day (11 April), but competitors must still carry breakfast and food for that stage. Food removed from its original packaging must carry a readable nutritional label; the first four meals on 3–4 April are outside this check. If the 2,000 kcal/day is not produced at a check, the regulation states a 2-hour penalty.

### The organiser supplies water, not food

Articles 16 and 13 of the 2026 regulations set out the arrangement:

- At each stage finish, every competitor receives **5 litres of water**;
- The next water is at the **first checkpoint of the following day**;
- Each stage has **2–8 checkpoints**, which supply water and have medical support;
- Competitors may not take water themselves or accept outside assistance;
- The minimum water-container capacity is **1.5 litres**.

So the answer to "do aid stations give water or everything?" is: **water only, no food**. Food is carried by the competitor throughout. The organiser does not prescribe a minimum daily drinking volume; 1.5 litres is a container requirement and 5 litres is the stage-finish allocation, neither of which is a "daily minimum intake". This page does not turn the container capacity into drinking advice.

### Which stages are self-supported

All six race stages are food self-supported; none supplies food. The only difference is water distribution: 5 litres at each stage finish and more at the first checkpoint the next day. The rest day has no stage and no allocation.

### Carried weight

Article 24 of the 2026 regulations requires mandatory equipment and personal effects to weigh **at least 6.5 kg and at most 15 kg**, excluding daily water. The list includes a 20 L backpack, sleeping bag, a headlamp of at least 400 lumens with spare batteries, 1.5 litres of water capacity, a compass, whistle, metal knife, survival blanket, signal mirror, sunscreen and two bouillon cubes per day. **Gas stoves are explicitly forbidden.**

The second photograph, from 2004, shows a different surface: open, flat and almost without shade, with one runner carrying the load alone. It is not a 2026 course image either; the file page gives the race and date but no location, so it cannot establish whether a particular stage is sand, salt flat or dry lakebed.

## Bags and stage-to-stage luggage

In multi-day racing, how the bag moves can matter more than how fast you run. The MDS rule is direct:

> Pendant la course, aucun objet de votre sac à dos ne sera pris en charge par l'organisation.

In other words, **during the race the organisation takes charge of no item in your backpack**. There is no "bag truck to the next camp"; food, sleeping bag, headlamp and spare clothing are all carried. This is one of the biggest mechanical differences from multi-day races that transfer luggage between camps.

The 2026 regulations also say that after a withdrawal the competitor's "sac personnel" (personal bag) is returned at Ouarzazate. That refers to post-withdrawal personal luggage, not in-race transfer. The organiser publishes no pre-race bag-handover details; this page does not invent them.

## Medical and safety

### Official medical cover

Article 17 of the 2026 regulations says the medical team is chosen by the organiser and specialises in sports and tropical medicine; it patrols the course in rescue vehicles and is stationed at checkpoints and stage finishes; it may remove a competitor who is physically or mentally unfit to continue and has the authority to withdraw the bib and eliminate them. Every checkpoint has medical cover (Article 13).

### Heat, salt, sand and navigation

- **Heat:** Article 21 of the 2026 regulations gives April averages of **30°C** by day and **14°C** at night, marked "à titre indicatif". The regulations do not set out heat-illness recognition or cooling procedures; that is unknown here. General sports-medicine advice is not written up as a race rule.
- **Salt:** The regulations require two bouillon cubes per day "pour favoriser la réhydratation". The organiser does not frame this as hyponatraemia prevention; this page records the equipment requirement and does not extend it into a medical conclusion.
- **Sand:** Article 21 says that in a sandstorm with no visibility competitors must stop on the course axis and wait for instructions from the race marshals. That is a rule, not advice.
- **Navigation:** Competitors must follow markers, use the roadbook and check in at checkpoints; the mandatory kit includes a 1–2° compass and the organiser issues a GPS beacon. Leaving the course and triggering a long search carries a €200 fine in the regulations; outside assistance is strictly prohibited (Article 19).
- **Withdrawal:** A competitor must inform the organisation and sign the official discharge; leaving unannounced is not allowed. The medical team may withdraw the bib of a competitor unfit to continue.

This section sticks to what the official rules support. General sports-medicine guidance that the organiser does not state is not presented as a race rule.

## Arrival and assembly

The official 2026 programme is: on 3 April a bus transfer into the desert bivouac; on 4 April technical, administrative and medical checks; on 11 April, after stage 6, a bus back to Ouarzazate and a hotel; on 12 April a free day, awards and dinner; on 13 April departure transfers.

The official registration package states that transfers run **Marrakech airport ↔ Ouarzazate ↔ bivouac**, with two hotel nights in Ouarzazate after the race (classic or superior, the superior option being Berbère Palace). The official Legendary 2027 page says transfers leave Marrakech on 31 March and 1 April, the return bus leaves Ouarzazate at 06:00 on 12 April, and a Marrakech return flight should be booked after 14:00; runners flying directly into Ouarzazate reach their hotel on their own. Ouarzazate is the assembly city named in the official programme, not the race start; the start and each stage's start/finish stay secret until the roadbook is issued.

## Season and weather

Open-Meteo's Ouarzazate town record for 7–17 April, 2015–2025 (121 days): daily maximum 15.0–33.1 °C, median 26.8 °C; daily minimum 2.6–16.5 °C, median 10.0 °C; 4 of 121 days had at least 1 mm precipitation, and all 4 had at least 5 mm.

This is the Ouarzazate town grid, not the Sahara bivouac south of M'Hamid or the stage route. April desert surface temperature can far exceed air temperature, and nights and early mornings cool quickly; the problem is midday exposure, sand reflection, sustained dry heat and overnight recovery, not a single average. The organiser publishes no race-period bivouac measurements, so this page does not present town data as camp data.

## Difficulty

This is not an altitude problem: the six stages total 2,567 m of gain and the organiser does not single out a high point. The difficulty is duration, heat, sand, self-sufficiency, navigation and multi-day recovery. The official 2027 event page gives historical averages of a 90% finish rate, 5.2 km/h average speed and cut-offs set at 3.5 km/h, and states that over 30% of participants walk. This page does not merge the 2026 270.0 km with the 2027 "about 250 km" into one number.

## Training conversion: multi-day desert stage preparation

The MDS challenge is consecutive days, self-carried food, sand and sustained dry heat, navigation and the cross-night long stage (Stage 4, 100 km). Preparation centres on multi-day endurance, loaded run-walk and heat adaptation, not a single-day peak time. General methods are in [ultra-trail training plan](/en/knowledge/trail-ultra-training-plan/), [long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/), [heat and humidity](/en/knowledge/heat-and-humidity/) and [load and overtraining](/en/knowledge/load-and-overtraining/).

| Phase | Focus | Practical content | Race-day constraint |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T-20 to T-14 weeks | Aerobic and loaded base | Repeated loaded jog/walk sessions; build sun-exposed training | 6.5–15 kg pack, repeated over multiple days |
| T-14 to T-8 weeks | Back-to-back days | Back-to-back long runs or two-day run-walk sessions | Six stages over several days; Stage 4 at 100 km |
| T-8 to T-3 weeks | Self-sufficiency and heat adaptation | Test 2,000 kcal/day food carry and dehydrated fueling; one warm long run | Food carried throughout, water only from the organiser; midday exposure |
| T-3 to race | Taper | Shorten distance, keep rhythm; no new kit | Pass the pre-race medical check; see [taper and strength](/en/knowledge/strength-and-taper/) |

Boundary: this is a generic preparation framework, not medical advice. Runners with pre-existing cardiopulmonary conditions, a heat illness history or recent injuries should consult a doctor first.

## 1. 26.2 miles and a 10.55 km loop

The official distance is 26.2 miles, the standard 42.195 km marathon. The race is not on King George Island near the Antarctic Peninsula; it is in the interior at Union Glacier. The official site stresses “not King George Island, outside the Antarctic Circle”, which is the fundamental difference from Antarctic half-marathons and peninsula events.

The FAQ is specific about the course: normally a 10.55 km loop repeated four times, an aid station every 5 km, and a large heated structure at halfway.

## 2. The venue: Union Glacier camp at 80°S

Union Glacier camp is in the southern Ellsworth Mountains on the Antarctic interior, at 79°46′S 82°52′W, about 700 m (2,297 ft) above sea level. It is not a peninsula-edge experience: runners fly from Punta Arenas, Chile, over the Strait of Magellan, Tierra del Fuego and the Drake Passage for about four hours before landing on a blue-ice runway.

The official event page says the course is snow and ice throughout, the average windchill is about -20 °C, katabatic winds can reach 10–25 knots, and the camp is at about 700 m. It also says not to expect a personal best, penguins or crowds. That is closer to the race than any scenery copy: white, empty, cold, windy, and four repeated laps.

## 3. Four laps and splits: two aid stations per lap, one heated tent at halfway

The official site gives no per-lap profile, but it does give the structure:

- **Loop:** 10.55 km, four times; start/finish near camp.
- **Aid:** every 5 km, so roughly one at km 5 and one at km 10 of each lap; about eight aid visits across four laps, plus the finish.
- **Halfway:** a large heated structure near the end of lap 2 for shelter, layer changes and hand/face checks.
- **Marking:** flags guide runners, and participants should be visible almost all the time; visibility is usually manageable, but whiteout, drifting snow and a flat white surface can destroy distance judgement.
- **Surface:** groomed before the race, usually reasonably solid ice, but soft loose snow in places; trail shoes are recommended over road racing shoes.
- **Direction:** the official site does not say clockwise or counter-clockwise; this page does not guess and the entity records “loop direction not published”.

Four laps have a different psychology from a point-to-point: lap one is excitement, lap two starts counting aid stations, lap three becomes white noise, lap four is heat management and willpower. Break each lap into “aid at 5 km — aid at 10 km — back to camp” rather than staring at 42 km.

## 5. Gear: three layers, two base layers, no road racing shoes

The official gear page describes a layering system, not a brand list:

| Area | Official recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Upper body | Base layer (wicking) + mid fleece (insulation) + outer windproof shell |
| Legs | Base layer + windproof pants; only two layers, or you overheat |
| Hands | Glove liners + gloves + mittens + hand warmers |
| Feet | Two pairs of wool socks + sock liners; trail running shoes |
| Head/face | Balaclava, facemask, hat, neck gaiter, ski goggles or sports sunglasses |
| Spares | Two sets of leg/torso base layers, extra fleece, extra gloves and socks |

The official page stresses staying dry: if you sweat through, change into dry base layers during the race. That is why two sets are required, not one worn all day. At -10 to -20 °C the danger is not just “feeling cold” but sweating, stopping and cooling quickly in wind; layer changes usually happen at the halfway heated tent or aid stations.

## 6. Weather and environment: -10 to -20 °C, windchill about -20 °C

The FAQ says the temperature at Union Glacier typically ranges from **-10 °C to -20 °C** at race time; the event page says the average windchill is about **-20 °C**, with katabatic winds of 10–25 knots. Keep air temperature and windchill separate:

- **Air temperature:** -10 to -20 °C.
- **Windchill:** wind pushes the felt temperature to about -20 °C, lower in stronger wind.
- **Precipitation:** the Antarctic interior is a polar desert, but drifting/blowing snow can still cut visibility; it does not mean heavy snowfall.
- **Sun:** December is polar day, 24-hour daylight; snow reflection makes goggles and sunscreen non-optional.
- **Altitude:** about 700 m, not a high-altitude risk, but cold still raises cardiovascular load.

The official site publishes no hourly history or course microclimate, and this page does not substitute another station's data for Union Glacier. Prepare for cold + wind + dry + strong reflection, not for “Antarctica must be a blizzard”.

## 7. Travel and logistics: Punta Arenas is the only gateway

Everyone reaches Punta Arenas, Chile, then flies to Antarctica on the event charter:

1. **International:** fly to Santiago, then a domestic flight to Punta Arenas; the FAQ mentions Lan Chile, but airlines and schedules change, so check current operators.
2. **Punta Arenas:** official hotels are Diego de Almagro and Almasur; other hotels run USD 50–300 per night. Budget for several days of possible delay.
3. **Antarctic leg:** Boeing 757 charter, about four hours, over the Strait of Magellan, Tierra del Fuego and the Drake Passage, landing on the Union Glacier blue-ice runway near 80°S.
4. **Baggage:** 25 kg (55 lb) on the Punta Arenas–Union Glacier flight; excess is at your cost. Cameras, spare clothing and sleeping kit all count.
5. **Camp:** tented accommodation and meals are provided; the camp has a basic medical clinic, doctor/medic, 24-hour support and Starlink internet (speed weather-dependent).

![The blue-and-white Union Glacier camp buildings, snow tracks and a low ridge beyond under a pale blue sky; no start arch, timing mat or runners](/media/routes/antarctic-ice-marathon/antarctic-union-glacier-hq-2022.jpg)

*Figure 5 | Union Glacier camp buildings and tracks (31 December 2022, 19:24, Christopher Michel, [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)). The photograph shows the camp buildings and snow logistics setting only; it is not the start line, a timing mat, an aid station or a 2026 race-day photograph.*

The real travel risk is not getting lost but the flight slipping. Book the international return after 15 December and allow at least two weeks of buffer, as the official FAQ advises.

## 8. Difficulty and risk: cold, wind, white, repetition

| Risk | Presentation | Management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypothermia | Sweat, stop, wind, rapid cooling | Layers, dry base layers, heated tent |
| Frostbite | Fingers, toes, ears, nose, cheeks | Gloves + mittens, facemask, goggles, checks |
| Blowing snow/whiteout | Visibility drop, loss of direction | Follow flags, stay with others, wait at aid |
| Soft snow | Sinking steps, pace collapse | Trail shoes, shorter stride, time not pace |
| Four-lap psychology | Repetition, emptiness, no crowd | Split into segments, checks each lap |
| Delay | Antarctic flights cannot be guaranteed | Two-week return buffer, delay insurance |
| Medical distance | Nearest hospital in Punta Arenas | Camp clinic first, evacuation protocol |

The official FAQ says flags guide runners and participants are visible almost all the time, which lowers the “getting lost” risk but does not remove it: in whiteout, a flag can disappear at 10 m; four laps can hide accumulating fatigue; cold suppresses thirst, so dehydration and thick blood are easy to miss.

## Training conversion: from road running to the Antarctic loop

The target of this race's preparation is not a PB; it is to make body and kit last ten hours in cold, hard ice, soft snow and four repeated laps. Treat preparation in three layers: aerobic base, cold tolerance, and kit/fueling practice. A regular city runner following a marathon build-up to a 30–35 km long run already meets the aerobic requirement; what is easily underestimated is completing a long run in full layered clothing in the cold, and practising a mid-run base-layer change. General principles are covered in [cold-weather running](/en/knowledge/cold-weather-running/), [long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/) and [recovery days](/en/knowledge/recovery-day/); weekly structure and load control in [training week](/en/knowledge/training-week/) and [load and overtraining](/en/knowledge/load-and-overtraining/).

| Phase | Focus | Practical content | Race-day principle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T-12 to T-8 weeks | Aerobic base | 3–4 easy runs a week plus one long run; no speed work | The ten-hour cut-off allows an average pace of about 14:14/km; run-walk covers it |
| T-8 to T-4 weeks | Long run and kit trial | Extend the long run to 30–35 km; trial the full layering system in the cold and practise changing layers when sweating | The rules require two base layers; changes happen at aid stations or the heated tent |
| T-4 to T-1 weeks | Taper and adaptation | Shorten distance, keep some rhythm; sort mandatory and backup kit against the 25 kg baggage limit | Recover fatigue before Antarctica; see [taper and strength](/en/knowledge/strength-and-taper/) |
| Race week | Arrival and rest | Check kit per briefing while on standby in Punta Arenas; avoid extra fatigue | Flights may slip; keep a two-week buffer after the return |

Boundary: this is a generic preparation framework, not medical advice. Runners with pre-existing cardiopulmonary conditions, a history of frostbite or recent injuries should complete a pre-race medical check and consult a doctor before entering. This page does not replace clinical diagnosis. Training load in the cold cannot be extrapolated from room-temperature performances.

## Race-day execution card: from briefing to the cut-off

On race day the priorities are heat management, station rhythm and red-flag recognition, not pace confrontation. Before the start, work through the [race-morning protocol](/en/knowledge/race-morning-protocol/) and the [running checklist tool](/en/tools/race-checklist/); the framework for deciding whether to drop is in [drop-out decision](/en/knowledge/dnf-decision-making/).

| Time point | Action | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Night before | Lay out two base layers, gloves, sock liners and hand warmers | Keep spare clothing in a bag accessible at camp; confirm bib and chip |
| 60 min before start | Complete the briefing kit check; put on upper-body layers | Confirm goggles, face mask, hat and neck gaiter; drink without over-drinking |
| 15 min before start | Warm up, move hands and feet | Note the start time; confirm the first 5 km station |
| Each lap at 5 km / 10 km stations | Stop for fluid, heat and a hands/feet check | Change a soaked base layer at the halfway heated tent; do not linger in the wind |
| End of lap 2 (heated tent) | Shelter, layer change, glove/mask check | Assess state; plan the rhythm for the last two laps |
| Laps 3–4 | Push by time, not pace | Shorten stride in soft snow; follow flags and pair up in whiteout |
| Finish | Keep warm, stretch, record finish time | Change into dry clothes promptly; report any frostbite or discomfort to the camp clinic |

| Red flag | On-site action | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persistent numbness or whiteness in fingers, toes, ears or face | Warm up at a station immediately and notify race staff | This page does not diagnose frostbite; the camp medic assesses |
| Dizziness, palpitations, cold sweat, blurred vision | Stop, shelter, seek help; call medical if needed | Recognition and call for cardiac emergencies follow on-site first-aid standards; this page gives no treatment plan |
| Whiteout/blowing snow obscures the flags | Wait at a station or move with a partner; do not scout alone | The loop direction is unpublished; off-course risk is judged by on-site officials |
| Fatigue or altered gait | Enter a station under the drop-out procedure and contact officials | Dropping is acceptable; the ten-hour cut-off is a hard rule, not a willpower test |

The camp has a basic clinic, doctors/medics and 24-hour staffing; serious cases follow the official evacuation procedure, and the nearest regular hospital is in Punta Arenas. This page does not give medical advice for participation and makes no promise about evacuation timing.

## Course changes

The 2026 official FAQ still describes four 10.55 km laps, a heated tent at halfway and aid every 5 km; the 2025 official results page places the race on the same Union Glacier loop. No public page between the two editions announces a new course, reroute or distance change, and the organiser publishes no course-change log. This page therefore confirms only that 2026 uses four 10.55 km laps; it does not claim the 2025 and 2026 geometry are identical, and it does not turn the roughly 5 m difference between 4×10.55=42.20 km and 42.195 km into an official measurement error. Only independent 2025 and 2026 GPS traces could upgrade "no published change" to "verified unchanged".

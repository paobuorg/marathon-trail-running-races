## What this race is

PAGODA 50-DAY is a loop that starts and finishes at Doi Inthanon National Park Headquarters, in the organiser's own words.

The official listing gives a start on Saturday 28 November 2026 at 07:00 and a maximum allowed race time of 16 hours 30 minutes.

The route passes Doi Hua Suea, Doi Pha Tang Peak and the Twin Royal Pagodas, described by the organiser as running before reaching the summit of Doi Inthanon.

The 2026 event is split into two weeks: Week 1 at Doi Inthanon National Park Office on 27-29 November, and Week 2 at Chiang Mai PAO Park on 3-6 December.

Week 1 also includes INTHANON 20 and a warm-up run.

This page covers only the Week 1 PAGODA 50-DAY; the distances and aid stations of Week 2 are out of scope.

## Called 50, measured at 57

The race is named PAGODA 50, while the race page gives the distance as 57 KM.

The same page gives 3,600 M+ of elevation gain, and its start time and time limit match the 57 km version.

The official time chart runs to 56.7 km at the finish, 0.3 km short of 57 KM.

This page uses the official 57 km on the card and lists 56.7 km and the GPX measurement in the body.

This is not a claim of a typo; it is a warning that estimating the distance from the name undercounts by about 7 km.

The organiser does not explain the 50 versus 57 difference on the page.

## The high point and the word "summit"

The race page says the route runs before reaching the summit of Doi Inthanon.

The Thai Department of National Parks describes Doi Inthanon as Thailand's highest peak at 2,565 m above mean sea level.

The official GPX tops out at 2,149 m, 416 m below that summit.

Read together, the only careful conclusion is that the official text says "summit" while the official track file does not reach 2,565 m.

### Regional climate (not race data)

The numbers below come from two independent sources and are regional climate data, not organiser-published figures and not a race-day forecast; they describe the usual state of Doi Inthanon and of Chiang Mai at the foot of the mountain around November.

The Department of National Parks page for Doi Inthanon National Park gives the park's elevation range as 400 to 2,565 m, with the summit the highest point in Thailand.

The same page divides the park by altitude into three belts: tropical below 1,000 m, subtropical from 1,000 to 2,000 m and temperate above 2,000 m.

The same page divides the year into summer from March to May, a rainy season from June to November and winter from December to February, and says the southwest monsoon brings moisture and rain clouds while the northeast monsoon brings cold and drought.

The same page gives the park's central region a year-round average of about 20 °C and an average of 15–17 °C in December and January.

The same page describes the upper highlands as wet and cold all year, with gale-force winds and subfreezing temperatures possible at the summit and 0–4 °C on frosty days in December and January.

The same page gives average annual rainfall of 2,000–2,100 mm.

The same page adds that above 1,800 m low cloud keeps the forest clouded and foggy for most of the year.

The same page lists Doi Hua Mot Luang at 2,330 m, Doi Hua Mot Noi at 1,900 m and Doi Hua Suea at 1,881 m; the route passes Doi Hua Suea, which sits in that above-1,800 m belt.

On that official reading, November is the last month of the park's rainy season, not the dry season; this describes the season division only and does not forecast 28 November.

The park page works at park and summit scale, while this page's GPX carries a maximum elevation tag of 2,149 m below the 2,565 m summit, so points along the route do not sit in one climate belt.

There is also a station reading at the foot of the mountain. JMA ClimatView's Chiang Mai station (48327, 18.77°N / 98.97°E, 313 m) gives 1991–2020 normals: a November mean temperature of 24.7 °C and November precipitation of 29.8 mm.

The same station's normals are 22.5 °C and 10.0 mm for December and 22.3 °C and 11.3 mm for January, cooler and drier than November.

The station sits at 313 m, below the park's 400 m lower bound and far below the route's high points, so its November figures cannot stand in for the summit or the course.

Chiang Mai's sub-30 mm November normal coexists with the park page's June–November rainy season and its year-round cloud above 1,800 m; the two are at different elevations and spatial scales and do not refute each other.

Neither source gives a November relative-humidity figure or a November mean temperature for the summit; this page does not extrapolate the Chiang Mai station to 2,565 m and does not apply a lapse-rate conversion.

The organiser still publishes no temperature, rainfall, humidity or historical weather statistics for 28 November, so none of the regional figures above is race-day information or a statement of what the weather "usually" is during the race.

What the official material does support is a different set of facts: the regulations require runners to handle night, wind, cold, fog, rain or snow alone at altitude.

The Thai Department of National Parks describes Doi Inthanon as cool and misty all year round, with hill evergreen forest around the summit.

Together these show that the climate risk here is not a single tropical heat story but low-altitude heat and humidity combined with high-altitude cold and mist.

The official gear page makes this explicit: the mandatory shell is a windproof jacket with hood and DWR protection, not a waterproof rain jacket.

The same page's recommended list includes warm gloves, spare warm clothing, long trousers or socks covering the legs, sunglasses, arm sleeves and anti-heat cream.

Warm and heat-related items on one list show that the organiser expects both conditions during the race.

The FAQ is more direct: the hot-weather and cold-weather kit decisions are made one or two days before the race after assessing the latest conditions.

The gear page adds that the organiser may add mandatory items according to the forecast, with the final list confirmed one or two days before the race.

The organiser publishes no temperature range, rainfall probability or felt-temperature conversion.

## Water, salt and cold: how far the official text goes

The official minimum is 1 litre of water carried at all times, in hydration packs or bottles, with water in plastic bags or ziplock bags forbidden.

Aid stations provide still water and sports or electrolyte drink for refilling bottles and hydration packs.

The regulations require runners to leave each aid station with enough food and drink to reach the next one.

The organiser publishes no sodium concentration, electrolyte content per litre or list of drink products by station.

The organiser also publishes no sweat-rate, sodium-loss or individual hydration formula.

This page therefore does not write "how much sodium per hour", because no such number exists in the official material.

For cold, the mandatory list includes a survival blanket of at least 1.4 m by 2 m, a windproof jacket, a warm long-sleeve layer and a headlamp.

The regulations place hypothermia risk inside the runner's commitment, requiring self-reliance in low temperature, strong wind, rain or snow.

The organiser gives no summit night temperature and no wind-chill index.

For heat, the recommended gear includes sunglasses, arm sleeves and anti-heat cream, and the FAQ accepts that a hot-weather kit may be required.

The organiser gives no heat index, no wet-bulb globe temperature and no heat-illness protocol.

This page can therefore state the official gear and rules only, and does not present general sports-medicine hydration, salt or cooling advice as race fact.

The conclusion for water, salt and cold is the same sentence: the organiser gives equipment and principles, not doses and thresholds.

## The hard rules in the official medical guidance

The regulations state that medical, paramedical, first-aid and guide staff, and anyone appointed by the race direction, may remove any participant deemed unable to continue.

The same section allows them to require any competitor to use mandatory equipment, to evacuate runners in danger and to refer runners to an appropriate care facility.

The regulations state that if a runner's condition justifies treatment via intravenous drip, the runner must withdraw from the race compulsorily.

That is a hard rule, not a recommendation.

The regulations state that runners must stay on the marked path, even when they sleep.

If a runner needs to nap on the route, the organiser strongly recommends the supplied sleep bib, which signals that the runner is resting and does not want to be disturbed.

The regulations state that some aid stations have rest areas with camp beds and blankets.

The organiser does not publish which aid stations have rest areas, nor how many beds exist.

The mandatory first-aid kit should include wound care and pain relievers, and the page states that NSAIDs are prohibited.

That is the opposite of the common trail kit containing ibuprofen, so the official list has to be checked item by item.

The organiser does not explain the medical reason for the NSAID prohibition.

The regulations state that there are several medical stations along the route, directly linked to the command centre, with medical staff at the command centre during the event.

The same section lists four ways to call for help: go to a medical station, call the command centre, ask another runner to call, or use the SOS function in the LiveRun app.

## Difficulty and risk

This is an editorial assessment, not an official rating.

The first source of difficulty is the ratio of distance to climb: 57 km with 3,600 m of gain averages about 63 m per kilometre.

That ratio is close to a mid-to-hard European 50 km mountain race and higher than most city-edge 50 km events.

The second source is the elevation span: GPX tags run from 813 m to 2,149 m, a single-climb rise of more than 1,300 m.

The organiser gives no altitude-acclimatisation advice and no altitude-illness protocol.

The third source is the cut-off structure: 8 hours at B3, 11 hours at B4 and 14 hours 30 minutes at B5, tightening the margin through the second half.

With no cut-off at the first two aid stations, the real filtering starts at 28.7 km.

The fourth source is withdrawal logistics: the regulations allow withdrawal only at a checkpoint, and the organiser publishes no transport capacity by point.

The regulations state that vehicles at some aid stations return dropouts to the finish, but not at all of them.

The organiser does not publish which stations have vehicles and which rely on event transport.

So "you can always drop out" is not supported by the official text for this race.

The fifth source is climate uncertainty: the organiser prepares both hot-weather and cold-weather gear and finalises the list one or two days out.

Runners therefore have to pack for both extremes rather than for one named season.

## What this course is

Hardrock 100 starts at the Silverton School Gym in Silverton, Colorado, and returns to the same town after a full loop of the San Juan Range. The organiser describes it as four legs linking the Lake City, Ouray, Telluride and Silverton areas. The route passes through Telluride, Ouray and the ghost town of Sherman, crosses thirteen major passes in the 12,000–13,000 ft band, and goes above 12,000 ft thirteen times. The high point is the 14,048 ft summit of Handies Peak; the low point is around Ouray, variously given as 7,678–7,680 ft. The finish is not a line but a ritual: runners “kiss the Hardrock”, a ram’s head painted on a block of mining debris.

This is not a course that a single track can replace. The organiser says the event is not orienteering, but the course is closed and runners must follow the prescribed route. The whole course is marked before the run, yet long road sections and maintained trails may not be marked at all; cross-country sections are marked more heavily. Marmots drag markers into dens, elk trample them into mud, and people move or remove them. Runners remain responsible for knowing the route, so owning the GPX is not a guarantee of navigation.

The direction reverses each year. The 2026 edition was clockwise. The 2027 edition will be counter-clockwise, held 9–11 July 2027 with a 06:00 start on 9 July. This page pins the 2026 clockwise version: the segment table, climb and descent all follow that direction. Do not mix 2026 and 2027 segments, and do not treat a track from one year as official geometry for another.

Entry and field size belong here too, in the organiser’s own terms: the application window is the first two weeks of October on UltraSignup, and the lottery is held on the first weekend of December. For 2026 the lottery drew 147 entrants from about 3,000 applicants, split 64 in the Finished pool and 83 in the Never Finished pool; women were 22.2% of applicants. The entry fee is 450 USD plus processing fees, with a 50% refund before 1 June and none after. The 2027 lottery is set for 5 December 2026. These numbers explain why this page treats course data and entry data as separate subjects.

The shape of the four legs matters more than the table. Pass names, mileages and elevations below come from the 2026 official course description:

- **Leg 1 (0–27.8 mi, Silverton → Telluride).** Leaving town, the route follows the Rainbow Trail along and above the highway for just under 2 mi, drops to cross Highway 550, then crosses Mineral Creek with a fixed rope. The organiser calls Mineral Creek the biggest water crossing on the course. The leg then climbs Cataract Putnam Pass (mile 7.2, 12,600 ft), Porcupine Cataract Pass (8.6, 12,230 ft), Grant Swamp Pass (14.9, 12,920 ft) and Oscar’s Pass (21.3, 13,140 ft), with aid at KT (11.5) and Chapman (18.1) before the descent into Telluride. By mile 27.8 the runner has already crossed above 12,000 ft four times and above 13,000 ft once.
- **Leg 2 (27.8–43.9 mi, Telluride → Ouray).** From Telluride’s paved streets the course climbs the Cornet Creek Trail through Liberty Bell Basin to Mendota Ridge, traverses a scree field and reaches Kroger’s Canteen (32.7 mi, 13,098 ft). The course description warns that space is very limited. After the descent from Virginius Pass, the route follows mining roads through Governor Basin to Governor (36.3 mi), then descends Camp Bird Road into Ouray (43.9 mi, 7,678 ft), the low point of the entire course. In the 2026 clockwise description, pacers may start at Ouray.
- **Leg 3 (43.9–72.0 mi, Ouray → Sherman).** Leaving Ouray, the Ouray Bear Creek Trail enters a steep, narrow gorge. The organiser warns of rockfall, huge drops on the right and difficult stream crossings after heavy rain, and tells runners to lean left. Engineer (51.9 mi, 11,798 ft) is a pack-in station at treeline; the route then climbs Oh! Point (53.3, 12,910 ft), drops to Grouse Gulch (58.2, 10,729 ft), crosses American Grouse Pass (60.7, 13,020 ft) into American Basin, and reaches the 14,048 ft summit of Handies Peak (63.5). The descent goes to Burrows (67.8, 10,588 ft), then along Cinnamon Pass Road to Sherman (72.0). This is the densest high-altitude block on the course: after the 13,020 ft American Grouse Pass, the route climbs to the 14,048 ft Handies summit and then descends to Burrows.
- **Leg 4 (72.0–101.8 mi, Sherman → Silverton).** From Sherman the route climbs the Cataract Gulch Trail past Cataract Pole Pass (77.2, 12,200 ft) to Pole Creek (81.5, 11,244 ft), then over Maggie/Pole Pass (85.4, 12,530 ft) to Maggie Gulch (86.5, 11,838 ft). It crosses Buffalo Boy Ridge (88.0, 13,214 ft) and Green Mountain / Stony Pass Ridge (89.5, 12,980 ft), reaches Cunningham (92.5) and descends into Silverton. The organiser warns that the top of Buffalo Boy Ridge can vary from year to year with snow and cornice conditions, and that parts of this section have a faint or nonexistent trail.

This page’s segment and aid tables use the 2026 official aid-table values and do not blend figures from different sources.

Several points need to be explicit:

- **Cumulative climb is not published.** The aid table gives a climb and descent for each segment, not a cumulative figure for each station. The table therefore says “cumulative not published” and puts the segment value in brackets; this page does not pass off its own running total as an official number.
- **Cut-offs are published at eight points only:** KT, Chapman, Telluride, Ouray, Grouse Gulch, Sherman, Cunningham and Silverton. The other six rows say “None” in the official table. This page renders those as “no published cut-off” and does not infer one.
- **Drop bags are accepted at seven points:** Chapman, Telluride, Ouray, Grouse Gulch, Sherman, Cunningham and Silverton. The aid-station page confirms that drop bags are allowed only for the seven aid stations designated on the chart.
- **Seven points have toilets:** Telluride, Ouray, Grouse Gulch, Burrows, Sherman, Cunningham and Silverton.
- **Six points have normal crew access:** Telluride, Ouray, Grouse Gulch, Sherman, Cunningham and Silverton. Chapman is “hike-in only” and does not count as normal drive-in crew access.
- **The 2027 pacer start points are a separate rule set.** The official pacer page says that in 2027 pacers are allowed from Grouse Gulch onward, meeting only at that station and the later crew-access points at Ouray, Telluride and Chapman, plus KT for a hike-in exchange only; runners over 60 may have pacers for the whole run. For 2026, use the 2026 manual and course description, and do not project the 2027 rule backwards.
- **Pack-in stations have a limited range.** The organiser lists Pole Creek, Engineer, Kroger’s and Putnam as Hike-In: supplies are carried in on volunteers’ backs, food variety is limited to the essentials of a typical aid-station menu, and there is no crew access. Do not treat these stations as a place to restock a full food plan.
- **Every aid station has at least an EMT, a first aid kit and radio communication.** That is the course page’s wording; the rules also give aid-station captains and medical staff the authority to pull a runner.
- **Aid is limited to the station area.** The rules prohibit stashing supplies and allow aid only within 400 yards of a designated aid station. Do not plan to meet a crew or pick up a hidden cache outside those limits.

## Surface and terrain

The organiser publishes no percentage breakdown for road, dirt trail, scree or cross-country, so this page does not invent one. What can be confirmed is the official description of the surface: a loop on 4WD mining roads, dirt trails and cross country, with the course “on trails as much as possible”. Long road sections and maintained trails may be unmarked, while cross-country sections are marked more heavily. That tells a runner that runnability and navigation difficulty vary sharply between sections, but it does not support a reliable percentage.

Altitude gives the course a second structure. The official range is about 7,700 ft to over 14,000 ft, with local timberline around 11,800 ft. Below timberline are aspen, pine and spruce forests; above it are alpine tundra and low vegetation interspersed with krummholz, the stunted spruce, fir and willow. The aid table’s spot elevations run from 7,678 ft at Ouray to 13,098 ft at Kroger’s, so most of the route is already at or above treeline; Handies Peak at 14,048 ft is the highest point. The course’s average elevation is over 11,000 ft, and it is exposed terrain, which is a different climate from the sheltered valley where the start and some aid stations sit.

The organiser also gives a concrete experience figure: in a typical year, runners’ feet are wet 90% or more of the run. That is a proportion of time, not a proportion of surface, and it cannot be used to estimate how much of the course is road or scree. The terrain warnings are equally direct: knee-deep ice-cold stream crossings, snow fields that are slick at night and post-holing by day, trails with cliffs immediately to one side, mild rock climbing that requires hands, and exposure above treeline in extreme thunderstorms.

## Risks and escape points

Hardrock’s risk is not a single hazard but the stacking of altitude, weather, remoteness and technical terrain:

- **Altitude.** The average elevation is over 11,000 ft, the course goes above 12,000 ft thirteen times and the high point is 14,048 ft. The organiser says altitude can cause altitude sickness or edema in some runners and is a major reason the average finishing time is close to 40 hours.
- **Weather.** The organiser treats weather as being at least as formidable as terrain, remoteness or elevation. The summer monsoon is a daily cycle: calm mornings, building thunderheads as the day warms, intense afternoon thunderstorms, then abating in the evening and through the night. Runners are told to carry gear for drenching rain, high winds and near-freezing temperatures, possibly all at once. The 48-hour cut-off exists partly so a runner can wait out a thunderstorm in a valley and still finish.
- **Lightning.** The organiser reports direct contact with lightning in past years and several near misses. If hair stands on end or skin tingles, a strike is imminent; the official lightning position involves spreading a group apart, putting feet together, crouching, minimising ground contact, covering ears and touching elbows to knees. This page repeats the official guidance rather than adding experience of its own.
- **Snow and scree.** Snow fields may be steep and extremely slick at night and post-hole above sharp scree by day; cornices can fall from above or collapse underfoot. The organiser asks runners to use caution when approaching snow-field edges from above or below.
- **Water and cliffs.** Mineral Creek is the biggest crossing and can be difficult in high snowmelt years and after heavy rain. The Ouray Bear Creek Trail enters a narrow gorge with rockfall, huge drops on the right and difficult stream crossings in heavy rain.
- **Remoteness and self-support.** Stretches between aid stations can be long; pack-in stations have a limited range; stashing is prohibited; aid is allowed only within 400 yards of a designated aid station; and pacers may not carry water, food, lights, shoes, clothing or other supplies for their runner (no muling).
- **Escape procedure.** The rules require a runner who drops to inform the nearest aid-station captain, have the ID bracelet cut and return the GPS tracker. The organiser also warns that arranging transport from a dropout point can be very hard, may take many hours and is only a best-effort promise. Search and rescue is initiated by the run director; every runner must carry the organiser-issued tracker; and initiating a rescue means the runner has dropped and cannot re-enter the run.
- **Vehicle access is not a guaranteed evacuation.** The aid table’s Access column divides points into Auto, 4WD and Hike. That is the first filter for “can a vehicle get here?”, but the organiser does not promise an immediate lift from every Auto point. A runner who needs to leave should follow the official dropout process rather than take a ride that advances progress; the rules prohibit vehicle rides that advance a runner or compensate for a navigation error.
- **Medical pull.** Run officials or medical staff may pull a runner who is significantly impaired, unable to make sound decisions or unable to continue safely, regardless of cause. Aid-station captains have authority to act for run management.
- **Closed course.** Cutting switchbacks is prohibited. A runner who deviates must backtrack to the point of departure before continuing. That rule matters most on the long, unmarked road sections.

## Getting there

The organiser does not publish a standalone travel page. This page separates what can be verified into air, road, rail and in-town movement, and states plainly where the official material stops.

- **Air.** Durango-La Plata County Airport (DRO) is the primary regional airport for southwest Colorado, northwest New Mexico and the Four Corners region. Its official site says American Airlines and United Airlines serve DRO, with year-round daily nonstop service to Dallas-Fort Worth (DFW), Denver (DEN) and Phoenix (PHX), plus seasonal nonstop service to Houston (IAH). The airport lists rental cars and a ground-transportation page, and says long-term parking is a few steps from the terminal.
- **Road.** Silverton sits on the US 550 corridor. The 2026 course description’s first leg crosses Highway 550 within the opening miles, after following the Rainbow Trail along and above the highway for just under 2 mi. That highway is the main link toward Durango to the south and Ouray to the north. Summer is road-construction season in the high mountains, so check CDOT and other current sources. The 2025 manual is more specific: it lists summer single-lane closures on US 550 between Silverton and Durango and up to 15-minute traffic holds on US 550 between Silverton and Ouray. Those are 2025 plans and must be rechecked against current CDOT notices. The official pages reviewed here do not give a specific driving distance or time from Silverton to Durango or Ouray.
- **Rail.** The Durango & Silverton Narrow Gauge Railroad has operated continuously between Durango and Silverton since 1882 and is a National Historic Landmark. Its official site says round-trip Silverton excursions depart from the Durango Depot and begin on 2 May. This is a scenic railway, not a race shuttle. Race-day operation, luggage capacity and whether it can carry running gear must be checked against the current timetable; it should not be treated as a guaranteed way to reach the start.
- **Start and in-town movement.** The start, finish, runner check-in and mandatory briefing are at the Silverton School Gym on 12th Street between Snowden and Reese. Drop bags and Camping Hardrock are at the Kendall Mountain Rec Center, 1 Kendall Pl on the east side of town across the 14th Street bridge. Public showers are at Anesi Park on Blair Street between 12th and 13th; the official lodging page says they are open 7a–8p daily, cost 5 USD for a five-minute token and accept cash only.
- **Lodging.** The official lodging page describes Camping Hardrock as a free community option at the Kendall Mountain Recreation Area. It has room for 180 vehicles and tents, with RVs, Sprinters and trailers limited to 30, and pre-registration is required. The ski lodge is open for limited general use including water, bathrooms and electricity. Registration for 2027 opens in May 2027. The same page notes that Ouray faces less lodging pressure than Silverton and links to Silverton Chamber of Commerce listings, Cascade Village and Purgatory Resort.
- **Driving and crew limits.** The 2026 manual says Grouse Gulch crew vehicles must be held at the Eureka Campground and are released only after their runner passes Oh! Point at about mile 53. Telluride crews may no longer park in the Town Park lot and should use the Silverjack and Shandoka lots. Ophir Pass Road bans stopping, spectating, media and drop-offs along its length. The course description adds that the roads around Grouse Gulch are rough and narrow, with 4WD strongly recommended.
- **No official airport shuttle.** The official pages reviewed here do not list an airport-to-Silverton shuttle or fixed bus service. What is confirmed is that DRO has rental cars and ground-transportation information, that a historic railway connects Durango and Silverton, and that US 550 is the road corridor. Any specific transfer must be arranged through the airport page and local operators; this page does not invent schedules or prices.

## Season and weather

The 2026 edition ran 10–12 July, starting at 06:00 on 10 July. To prepare for that window, this page queried the Open-Meteo historical archive for 10–12 July in each year from 2016 to 2025 at the Silverton grid point, giving 30 days:

- Daily maximum temperature: 18.3°C to 26.4°C, median 22.1°C.
- Daily minimum temperature: 1.8°C to 11.4°C, median 6.3°C.
- 11 of the 30 days had at least 1 mm of precipitation, 5 had at least 5 mm, and the largest daily total was 14 mm.

The data basis matters. The requested coordinates were 37.811N / 107.664W; Open-Meteo returned the grid cell 37.7856N / 107.7108W at 2,838 m (about 9,311 ft) elevation, timezone America/Denver. These are **gridded historical statistics, not on-course measurements**, and they are not readings from a specific weather station in Silverton. The grid cell sits at 2,838 m, below the course’s average elevation of over 11,000 ft (about 3,353 m). Handies Peak at 14,048 ft (about 4,282 m) will be markedly colder, and wind, cloud, precipitation and thunderstorms will change the felt temperature further. The grid-cell minimum should not be used to decide high-altitude kit.

The organiser’s seasonal description supports the same caution: the summer monsoon builds thunderheads every afternoon; the 48-hour cut-off lets runners wait out a storm in a valley; feet are wet 90% or more in a typical year; and runners should prepare for rain, wind and near-freezing temperatures at the same time. With a 06:00 start, anyone finishing over 40 hours sees two sunsets. Night cold combined with altitude, not the valley daytime maximum, is the real weather risk.

## Difficulty rating

The organiser publishes no single difficulty grade, and this page does not invent an official one. The following is an **editorial rating**, based only on verifiable dimensions:

- 101.8 mi (about 163.8 km) with 33,441 ft of climb and the same descent.
- Average elevation over 11,000 ft, thirteen climbs above 12,000 ft, a high point of 14,048 ft and a low point around 7,678–7,680 ft.
- A 48-hour cut-off, with the official average finishing time given as 39:52:11.
- Thirteen major passes in the 12,000–13,000 ft band, plus snow fields, scree, cliffs, water crossings and sections that require hands.
- Remoteness, pack-in aid stations, long aid spacing and strict self-support and no-muling rules.

The editorial judgment is that this is a high-altitude mountain ultra whose difficulty sits above a lowland 100-mile. The reason is not that the numbers look large, but that altitude, technical terrain, remoteness and the cut-off act together: an average finish close to 40 hours already sits near the upper end of most 100-mile cut-offs, and thirteen trips above 12,000 ft mean repeated exposure to high-mountain weather. That judgment is editorial, not an official grade, and it is not a prediction of any individual’s ability. For arithmetic, use the [pace calculator](/en/tools/pace/); for general ultra context, start with [the marathon distance](/en/knowledge/marathon-distance/).

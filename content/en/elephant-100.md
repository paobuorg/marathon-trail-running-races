## What this race is

ELEPHANT 100 is the 100 km race of Week 2 at Chiang Mai Thailand by UTMB. The official page lists it at 08:00 on 4 December 2026, starting at Wat Nhong Bua Noi and finishing at Chiang Mai PAO Park in Chiang Mai city.

The official limit is 28 hours, so the finish closes at 12:00 on 5 December.

The race page describes a point-to-point route through Wat Phra Phutthabat Si Roi, the Hmong village at Ban Doi Pui, the Doi Mon Jam viewpoint and Mae Sa Elephant Camp, before entering PAO Park.

The photograph shows the tropical valley setting around A13 Maesa Elephant Camp only; it is not the camp, an aid station, a 2026 course photograph or a race-day scene.*

The 2026 event runs over two weeks: Week 1 on 27-29 November at the Doi Inthanon National Park headquarters, Week 2 on 3-6 December at Chiang Mai PAO Park.

Week 2 also hosts CHIANG DAO 160, day and night HMONG 50 races, day and night SUTHEP 20 races, CHEDI 20 and a warm-up run.

The official announcement calls the whole event a UTMB World Series Major and says Running Stones are doubled for every race.

This race is listed with 6 Running Stones, and the Finals Access graphic is labelled 100k-direct-entry-major.

Both are official labels; this page does not interpret the entry rights behind them.

In the UTMB distance bands, 100 km sits in the 100K band, so this is the Major's 100K representative race. The 100M and 50K races are covered on the CHIANG DAO 160 and PAGODA 50-DAY pages.

## 100 km, 101 km and 5,500 m

The race page says 100 KM, and the official time chart's finish row says 101 km.

The official GPX measures 101.065 km in three dimensions and 99.586 km in two dimensions.

The three figures span 99.586 to 101.065 km, a range of 1.479 km; the organiser does not explain the 100 versus 101 difference.

The card uses the official 100 km, and the body lists 101 km and both GPX measurements without averaging.

On climb, the race page says 5,500 M+, the time chart sums to 5,540 m, the GPX yields 5,693 m of raw positive change and 5,480 m with a 3 m threshold. Subtracting consecutive GPX points gives a raw cumulative descent of −5,737 m; the highest point is 1,600 m and the lowest 319 m. Descent is a measured value, not an official published figure.

GPX elevation tags run from 319 m to 1,600 m. A15 Wat Doi Pui is the highest aid station on the chart at 1,370 m.

## The relationship with CHIANG DAO 160

The two GPX files share the A9 to A16 waypoints, with identical coordinates.

Their final points are also identical at 18.83102, 98.9675, the PAO Park finish.

Projected onto each track, A9 sits at 19.89 km here and 91.11 km on CHIANG DAO 160; A16 sits at 88.76 km here and 160.04 km on the 160.

So the two courses overlap in their later sections, but the overlap starts at a different point for each.

Neither race page states how many kilometres overlap, or whether the 100 km race is a shortened version of the 160.

This page therefore stops at "shared waypoints and shared finish" and does not infer design intent.

One practical consequence is that the 08:00 100 km race and the 10:00 160 km race meet on the shared section, so runners share the path with another field.

The organiser does not publish wave separation, passing rules or how the shared section is marked.

## Aid, support points and mid-race baggage

Aid stations here start at A18 and continue through A9 to A16, nine in total.

The official FAQ lists three support points for ELEPHANT 100: A9, A11 and A13. Support crews need a Support Pass to enter the support area.

The same FAQ states that mid-race baggage transfer is available only for CHIANG DAO 160 and ELEPHANT 100.

This race has a single transfer point: a Green Bag at A11.

The bag is issued at bib collection, only official event bags are accepted, trekking poles may not be placed inside, and runners may not put poles into it during the race.

The organiser publishes no capacity limit for the bag and no detailed list of what each aid station serves.

The regulations state that aid stations provide still water and sports or electrolyte drinks for refilling bottles and bladders, and that runners must leave each station with enough water and food for the next one.

## Mandatory kit: shared with the 160

The mandatory gear page lists equipment by race, and ELEPHANT 100 shares one 14-item table with CHIANG DAO 160, separate from the PAGODA 50 table.

The 14 items are a running pack, a mobile phone, a personal collapsible cup of at least 150 ml, at least 1 litre of water, one working headlamp with spare cells, a survival blanket of at least 1.40 m by 2 m, food, a waterproof jacket with hood and DWR protection, an LED or flashing light or a reflective vest or belt, a first-aid kit, a whistle, a cap, bandana or buff, a reusable bowl with a spoon or spork, and a passport or ID card.

The clearest difference from PAGODA 50 is the shell: PAGODA 50 requires a windproof jacket, while this table requires a waterproof one.

Bottles or flasks with lids are not accepted in place of the collapsible cup, and water may not be carried in plastic bags or ziplock bags.

Food is listed with a recommendation of 800 kcal, for example 2 gels and 2 bars; the FAQ states explicitly that the figure is a recommendation, not a requirement.

The first-aid kit must include wound care and pain relievers, and NSAIDs are prohibited.

The phone must stay on for the whole race with airplane mode forbidden, the LiveTrail app must be installed and running, and an external battery is highly recommended.

The recommended list adds trekking poles, a GPS watch, sunglasses, arm sleeves, warm gloves, spare warm clothing, long trousers or calf socks, Vaseline or anti-heat cream and THB 500 in cash.

The organiser may add items according to the weather forecast, with the final list confirmed one or two days before the race.

## Inconsistencies in the official text

First, headlamps. The gear table says one working headlamp, while the FAQ says CHIANG DAO 160 and ELEPHANT 100 runners must carry two working headlamps and two sets of spare batteries, one set per lamp.

Both are on the same official site. This page lists both and does not pick one; prepare to the stricter version.

Second, the app name. The gear table says LiveTrail and the runner-facing FAQ says LiveRun.

Third, identification. The gear table accepts a photocopy, while the FAQ says a photo of an ID card or passport on a phone is not accepted because rescue teams cannot access phone data.

Fourth, the medical centre. The regulations place it at the Betong Mongkolrit Tunnel start and finish; Betong is not in Chiang Mai, and this race starts at Wat Nhong Bua Noi and finishes at PAO Park.

Fifth, the distance: the race page says 100 km and the time chart's finish row says 101 km.

## Medical rules and safety

The regulations state that doctors, nurses, first responders and race control may remove any runner deemed unable to continue, may require the use of any mandatory item, may evacuate a runner in danger and may transfer a runner to a suitable medical facility.

They state that a runner who needs intravenous fluids must be withdrawn from the race.

They state that runners must stay on the marked route, including while sleeping, and strongly recommend the event sleep bib for a short sleep on course.

Some aid stations have rest areas with camp beds and blankets; the organiser does not say which stations, or how many beds.

The regulations list four ways to call for help: reach a medical point, call race control, ask another runner to call, or use the SOS function in the LiveRun app.

The number of medical points, ambulance response time and transfer time are not published; all three are unknown on this page.

On withdrawal, the regulations require runners to withdraw at a checkpoint, or to reach the nearest checkpoint if they are between stations. Transport capacity at each point is not published.

## Race-week weather: what is and is not published

The race runs from 4 to 5 December. The organiser publishes no temperature, rainfall, humidity or monsoon statistics for those dates and no weather record from past editions.

This page therefore does not present "cool season" or "dry season" as a race fact.

### Regional climate (not race data)

The Japan Meteorological Agency ClimatView station for Chiang Mai (station 48327, 18.77°N / 98.97°E, 313 m) gives 1991-2020 normals of 22.5°C mean temperature and 10.0 mm of precipitation for December, and 22.3°C and 11.3 mm for January.

November at the same station is 24.7°C and 29.8 mm, warmer and wetter than December.

The station sits at 313 m in Chiang Mai city, while the start of this race is around 710 m in the GPX and A15 sits at 1,370 m on the chart.

The December city figures therefore cannot stand in for A15 or the ridge sections, and this page does not apply a lapse rate.

The only direction the regional data supports is that early December in the city is cooler and drier than November; it cannot be read as "conditions during the race are usually...".

What the organiser does support is different: the regulations require runners to handle night, wind, cold, fog, rain or snow alone across altitude changes.

The gear page makes the point concrete by requiring a waterproof jacket for this race while PAGODA 50 requires only a windproof one.

No temperature range, rain probability or feels-like conversion is published.

## Travel and accommodation: the Week 2 gap

The start is at Wat Nhong Bua Noi and the finish is at Chiang Mai PAO Park, 27.0 km apart in a straight line between the two GPX end points.

The official travel page and FAQ describe the Week 1 venue at the Doi Inthanon National Park headquarters, including shops, restaurants, camping and gear rental within 500 m and lodging in Chom Thong about 30 km away.

None of that applies to Week 2, and this page does not move it to PAO Park.

The FAQ offers an airport shuttle from Chiang Mai International Airport and Chiang Mai city at THB 400.

The same entry still says the call centre is available from 7 December 2022 and links to the old inthanon.utmb.world domain.

The organiser publishes no runner shuttle between the Week 2 start and finish, no bag truck timetable, no parking plan and no return transport; all are unknown here.

What can be confirmed is that the finish is in Chiang Mai city, closer to the airport and city lodging than the Week 1 headquarters.

## Entry and fees

The early-bird entry fee for the 2026 ELEPHANT 100 is THB 6,500.

The organiser states that this is an early-bird price and that it rises automatically once a set number of entries is reached.

The race page gives only the 6 Running Stones and 100k-direct-entry-major labels and no UTMB Index threshold, so this page does not state an Index requirement.

Age groups on the regulations page run from 16-17 to 85 and over, split by gender.

Whether ELEPHANT 100 accepts the 16-17 group is not stated separately, and this page does not infer it.

The regulations allow a personal assistant only at specific aid stations in the 100M, 100K and 50K races, carrying one bag of no more than 30 litres.

This race is in the 100K band and can fall under that rule, but the organiser does not list which stations allow an assistant.

## Difficulty and risk

This is an editorial judgement, not an official rating.

First, the distance-to-climb ratio: 100 km with 5,500 m averages about 55 m per kilometre.

That is slightly gentler than CHIANG DAO 160 at about 57 m per kilometre, but 5,500 m over 100 km is still a mid-mountain 100 km course.

Second, the altitude profile: A9 at 1,060 m, A12 at 1,280 m and A15 at 1,370 m form three high sections, separated by low points at A13 (520 m) and A10 (730 m).

Repeated climbing and descending costs more than one long climb, and the chart's 5,540 m cumulative ascent shows that net gain understates the load.

Third, the cut-off structure: 3.5 hours for the 10 km from A10 to A11, 8 hours for the 32 km from A13 to A16, and 2.5 hours for the last 11 km.

Fourth, the chart's internal conflict: the A16 Slowest time of 09:43 is later than the 09:30 cut-off, which means the official slow model is already over the limit at that point.

That does not prove the barrier will be enforced at 09:43, but runners should not treat 09:43 as available margin.

Fifth, the shared course: the A9 to A16 section is shared with the 160 km race, so the 100 km field meets slower runners late in the race, and the organiser publishes no passing or marking rules for the overlap.

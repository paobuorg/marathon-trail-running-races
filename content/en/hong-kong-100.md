## What this course is

The Hong Kong 100 (HK100 Ultra Marathon) starts at Pak Tam Chung in Sai Kung, in the eastern New Territories, and runs west along the MacLehose Trail and its scenic diversions. It crosses coastal paths, beaches, old villages, reservoirs and ridgelines, then finishes with the descent from Tai Mo Shan (957 m), Hong Kong's highest peak, and about 4 km of downhill running into Rotary Club Park on Tai Mo Shan Road. The organiser defines it precisely: a 100 km trail race built around the MacLehose Trail, with diversions added so that runners spend more time in the trail's most scenic sections. It is not a road marathon moved onto hills.

The race was first held in January 2011 with 175 starters. It was founded by two Hong Kong lawyers, Janet Ng and Steve Brammar. By 2026 the field had grown to 1,844 finishers, making it one of Asia's largest hundred-kilometre trail races and the opening event in the 2027 World Trail Majors calendar. The 2027 race week runs from 21 to 24 January and contains four categories:

- The Third: Thursday 21 January, 34 km, 1,558 m of climb, 10-hour limit.
- The Half: Friday 22 January, 53 km, 2,263 m of climb, 14-hour limit.
- HK100: Saturday 23 January, 100 km, 5,142 m of climb, 30-hour limit.
- The Grand Sam: 21–24 January, all three races back to back, 183 km, 8,963 m of climb, 54-hour limit.

Those four sets of figures come from the 2027 race cards on the organiser's home page. The Third and The Half both use the first part of the HK100 course: The Third finishes with the climb to Tai Cham Koi and its 360-degree view of the Sai Kung Peninsula, while The Half covers roughly the first 53 km. In practice the first 44–53 km of HK100 are shared with the other two races: the transport page notes that the start and CP1 to CP5 also apply to The Half, and the start, CP1 and CP2 to The Third.

The geography is worth setting out because it explains the shape of the race. The first half follows the Sai Kung coast. The first 10 km are on Stage 1 of the MacLehose Trail, with the islands of the South China Sea on the right and High Island Reservoir on the left; the route crosses the dams that form the reservoir to CP1 at East Dam. It then climbs Sai Wan Shan, descends to Sai Wan village, crosses Sai Wan and Ham Tin beaches, skirts behind Ham Tin village to Tai Long Wan, turns inland about halfway along Tai Long Wan beach, and after a stretch of "jungle trail" rejoins Stage 2 of the MacLehose Trail. The middle section climbs from Pak Tam Au over Ngau Yee Shek Shan, descends the Cheung Sheung country trail to Hoi Ha Road, follows the road to Pak Sha O, then uses fast coastal path and dirt trail to Yung Shue O before climbing Kai Kung Shan to Kei Ling Ha. The second half runs from Kei Ling Ha over the Ma On Shan ridge, behind Lion Rock and over Beacon Hill into northern Kowloon, then down to Shing Mun, Golden Hill, Needle Hill, Grassy Hill and finally Tai Mo Shan. The organiser states plainly that most of the climbing is in the second half, so the first half has to be paced with something left in reserve.

Entry is capped by the Hong Kong Government: 1,800 for HK100, 500 for The Half and 500 for The Third. Places go through a ballot, with exemptions only for past winners, 2026 volunteers, runners who entered all 16 editions from 2011 to 2026, and sponsor nominees. Ballot results for the 2027 edition were released on Monday 17 August 2026; successful entrants had to complete registration by Sunday 23 August 2026, and unclaimed places were reallocated from Monday 31 August 2026.

## Official figures

Hong Kong 100 does not use one single distance and climb figure everywhere, and no official page publishes a descent total. The table below sets out the official figures side by side without choosing a favourite:

| Basis | Distance | Climb | Descent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Organiser's 2027 race card | 100 km | 5,142 m | Not published |
| Organiser's HK100 course page text (2027) | 100 km | over 5,000 m | Not published |
| UTMB Index race page (2026 edition, as run) | 92.9 km | 5,073 m | Not published |

(Only the 2027 course page states a high point: Tai Mo Shan at 957 m; neither the race card nor the UTMB Index page lists one.)

The card at the top of this page uses the 2027 race card's **100 km / 5,142 m**, because that is the organiser's headline pair for the 2027 edition and the only place where both a distance and a climb figure are stated together. The course page's "100km journey climbs over 5,000m" describes the same edition at lower precision; it does not contradict 5,142 m.

The third row needs its own explanation. UTMB Index records 92.9 km and 5,073 m for the 2026 edition. That is not a shorter 2027 course: it is the shortened course actually used in January 2026. Third-party race history records that Typhoon Wipha damaged the route in the summer before the race and that the January 2026 course was shortened by roughly 7 km. The 2026 finishing times and climb therefore cannot be compared directly with the 2027 100 km version, and 92.9 km must not be presented as "the official distance".

There is also a small internal inconsistency in the official text. At CP10 (Lead Mine Pass, 87 km) the course page says runners have "just 10km to go", but the checkpoint's cumulative distance of 87 km against a 100 km finish gives 13 km. The same passage says there is a 4 km run downhill after the Tai Mo Shan summit. Both statements come from the same official page; this page reports them and flags the difference rather than rewriting the organiser's text. The segment table therefore derives distances by subtracting consecutive official cumulative kilometre marks, which makes CP10 to the finish 13 km.

No official page publishes a descent total, and the official GPX description does not either. All three tables on this page therefore say "not published" for descent. Any statement that descent is "roughly equal to climb" would be this site's own inference and is not used as official data.

Segment notes. Place names, hill names and terrain descriptions are taken from the 2027 official course page:

- **Segment 1 (0 → 10 km, East Dam).** Stage 1 of the MacLehose Trail, with the South China Sea islands on the right and High Island Reservoir on the left, crossing the dams to East Dam. CP1 sits at East Dam; the road to East Dam is closed on race days and no vehicles can reach the checkpoint. This is the smoothest and fastest running of the whole course, but because the organiser puts most of the climbing in the second half, pacing discipline here matters more than speed.
- **Segment 2 (10 → 17 km, Sai Wan Pavilion).** A climb over Sai Wan Shan to Sai Wan Pavilion. For 2027 the organiser has relocated CP2 to Sai Wan Pavilion, before the beaches; the course page explicitly labels this as a 2027 change. CP2 positions in 2026 and earlier material therefore do not apply to 2027.
- **Segment 3 (17 → 28 km, Pak Tam Au).** A short climb gives views over Sai Kung Country Park, then the route descends to Sai Wan village, crosses Sai Wan and Ham Tin beaches, skirts behind Ham Tin village to Tai Long Wan, turns inland about halfway along the beach, follows a stretch of "jungle trail", rejoins Stage 2 of the MacLehose Trail and undulates to Pak Tam Au. CP3 is also relocated to Pak Tam Au for 2027. This segment contains the course's best-known beach scenery, and also the most obvious humidity and soft-sand cost.
- **Segment 4 (28 → 36 km, Pak Sha O).** From Pak Tam Au the route climbs steeply over Ngau Yee Shek Shan, with views to islands including Kau Sai Chau on the left at the summit. After the descent it turns sharply right at Cheung Sheung and drops down the Cheung Sheung Country Trail, which the organiser calls a soft and bouncy trail runner's dream. It turns left at Hoi Ha Road and follows the road to Pak Sha O. The organiser specifically requires runners to use the pavement and cross the road with care. CP4 is at Pak Sha O.
- **Segment 5 (36 → 44 km, Yung Shue O).** Pak Sha O to Yung Shue O mixes fast coastal path with dirt trail. Approaching the checkpoint, runners look across Kei Ling Ha towards Ma On Shan ("Saddle Hill"). It is short, and it is the last cheaply fast section before the second-half climbing begins.
- **Segment 6 (44 → 52 km, Kei Ling Ha).** The organiser states that after CP5 the real climbing starts. The route leaves via the back of Yung Shue O village, climbs the Cheung Sheung Country Trail, turns sharply right to rejoin the MacLehose Trail, and crosses Kai Kung Shan ("Rooster Hill") before descending to Kei Ling Ha. CP6 is the only mid-course checkpoint with drop bags and marks the practical split between the two halves of the race.
- **Segment 7 (52 → 65 km, Gilwell Camp).** The organiser calls this the race's longest and hardest stage and tells runners to make sure they have enough liquids. The ridge after Ma On Shan gives views over Sai Kung and the hundreds of islands of the South China Sea, followed by smooth, runnable orange-coloured dirt trail and the welcome at Gilwell Scout Camp. At 13 km, this segment combines distance, climbing and exposure.
- **Segment 8 (65 → 74 km, Beacon Hill).** From Gilwell Camp the route traverses behind Lion Rock, looking over Kowloon towards Hong Kong Island; the organiser calls it the best city view of the whole course. It then climbs to Beacon Hill. CP8 is generally accessible only on foot, about an hour's hike from the nearest road.
- **Segment 9 (74 → 80 km, Shing Mun).** A sharp descent into monkey country, then a stretch sharing the trails with macaques. Every runner must use the footbridge across Tai Po Road, which the organiser describes as required for both safety and fairness, before climbing the sealed road of Golden Hill. The organiser warns that monkeys will snatch food eaten here. CP9 is at Shing Mun Reservoir, reachable by car but with very limited parking.
- **Segment 10 (80 → 87 km, Lead Mine Pass).** Leaving Shing Mun, three climbs remain: the steep Needle Hill, the winding sealed road over Grassy Hill, and the sharp descent to Lead Mine Pass. CP10 is inaccessible to vehicles; the organiser's own advice is that it is easier to shuffle to the finish than to walk out of the country park.
- **Segment 11 (87 → 100 km, finish).** One last climb over Tai Mo Shan (957 m), then about 4 km downhill to Rotary Club Park. The official text says "just 10km to go" here, which differs by 3 km from the 13 km implied by 87 → 100; that discrepancy is noted above. Either way, the final 13 km still contains the highest point on the course, so CP10 is not a place to empty the tank.

The segment structure shows a clear rhythm. The first 52 km, up to CP6, are coastal paths, beaches, villages and country trails, with climbing spread in short pieces but coming often. After 52 km the route becomes a continuous sequence of ridges and sealed-road climbs: Ma On Shan, Lion Rock, Beacon Hill, Shing Mun, Needle Hill, Grassy Hill and Tai Mo Shan. That is where the organiser's "much of the climbing is in the second half" lands. CP6 is therefore not only a drop-bag point but the pacing and fuelling dividing line of the race.

Points that must be stated:

- **Supplies are not yet published.** The organiser has deferred the food and beverage list. This page therefore does not claim that a given checkpoint has hot food or drinks only. Preparation should follow the organiser's final checkpoint table, not an older edition's aid list.
- **Only two drop-bag points.** CP6 Kei Ling Ha and the finish. The organiser supplies two drawstring bags of 45 × 50 cm, one bag per person per drop point, deposited at the start on race morning. After a runner passes CP6, that bag is transported to the finish. Extra luggage costs HK$200 per item and goes directly to the finish, not to CP6.
- **First aid everywhere, escape capability not everywhere.** CP1 is behind a race-day road closure and CP10 has no vehicle access, so neither should be assumed to offer a quick lift out. CP8 and CP10 both require about an hour on foot to reach the nearest road.
- **Outside assistance only at checkpoints.** The rules permit support crews only at checkpoints; support outside checkpoints is not allowed except in an emergency. Buying drinks or food from shops, restaurants, hawkers and vending machines along the route is explicitly allowed.
- **Checkpoint refreshments are for runners only.** Support crews may not use them.
- **The 30-hour overall limit** comes from the 2027 race card. Per-checkpoint cut-off times were not published on the check date, and they must not be inferred from the overall limit.
- The rules require a runner who misses any cut-off to withdraw as instructed, and a runner who withdraws to report to the nearest checkpoint and sign the withdrawal form (unless first-aid crews remove them from the course).

## Surface and terrain

The organiser publishes no surface percentages and no "runnability" figures, so this page does not invent any. The terrain types that the official text does establish are:

- **Coastal path and hill trail.** The first 10 km are on Stage 1 of the MacLehose Trail, followed by Stage 2; the surface is predominantly dirt trail, stone steps and irregular hill path.
- **Beaches.** Sai Wan, Ham Tin and Tai Long Wan must be crossed; the organiser describes "crossing Sai Wan and Ham Tin Beaches" and turning inland about halfway along Tai Long Wan beach.
- **Jungle trail.** A stretch of "jungle trail" after Tai Long Wan before rejoining the MacLehose Trail.
- **Soft country trail.** The Cheung Sheung Country Trail is described by the organiser as soft and bouncy, one of the few non-paved sections explicitly called fast.
- **Orange dirt trail.** After Ma On Shan there is a smooth, runnable orange-coloured dirt trail along the ridge.
- **Pavement and road.** The Pak Sha O section requires the pavement and careful road crossing. The climbs over Golden Hill, Needle Hill and Grassy Hill include sealed road, which the organiser names explicitly.
- **Stone steps and steep ground.** The safety section states directly that the course is "rocky, uneven and steep in places" and that overtaking is only acceptable when it is safe.

The difficulty is not one long climb but the cumulative cost of constant surface change: soft sand, broken steps, slippery dirt, steep sealed road. Runners rarely settle into a steady road rhythm. The organiser gives no shoe or kit prescription, and this page does not substitute one.

## Risks and escape points

- **Night running.** HK100 takes place in late January with a 30-hour limit. Whatever the exact start time, the great majority of the field will see at least one night on the course. The mandatory kit requires a headlamp or torch with batteries, and this applies to HK100 and The Half. Headlamp, spare batteries and a spare light should be checked against the official list.
- **Heat and humidity.** January is Hong Kong's relatively dry season, but humidity can still be high. The course page repeatedly stresses drinking enough and carrying enough fluid to the next checkpoint. "It's January, it won't be hot" is not a reason to carry less water.
- **Technical ground and road crossings.** The organiser describes the course as rocky, uneven and steep in places. The Pak Sha O pavement and the Tai Po Road footbridge are mandatory. Crossing roads while running is a foreseeable risk on this course.
- **Monkeys.** Macaques around Shing Mun and Golden Hill will snatch food; the organiser calls them "cheeky, thieving, mischievous" and warns that eating there invites theft. Keep food in the pack, not in hand.
- **Mobile coverage.** The organiser states that most of the course has coverage but that some areas have none or only intermittent signal. A phone is not a guaranteed rescue channel for the whole route; the emergency numbers are printed on the race bib.
- **First aid and rescue.** Emergency first aid is available at every checkpoint. A runner needing help should call the event numbers on the bib so that the organiser can retrieve them. Walking off the course to find signal increases the risk of being unaccounted for.
- **Escape points.** According to the official transport page: CP1 has a race-day road closure and no vehicle access; CP2–CP4 are reachable by taxi or public transport, with private cars generally requiring a permit; CP5 by taxi, with private cars requiring a permit; CP6 has taxi and public transport but extremely limited parking; CP7 has taxi with very limited parking; CP8 is generally on foot only, about an hour; CP9 is car accessible with very limited parking; CP10 has no vehicle access and about an hour on foot. The genuinely fast escapes are concentrated in the first half plus CP6 and CP9. CP8 and CP10 are the places where walking out takes longer than continuing.
- **Withdrawal procedure.** Sign the withdrawal form at the nearest checkpoint; if first-aid crews remove a runner, they handle it. Leaving the course without notice is both a rule breach and a search-and-rescue problem.
- **Cancellation or course change.** The organiser reserves the right to cancel or shorten the event for adverse weather, landslides, rockfalls, hill fires, protests, blockage or force majeure, with no refund. January is not typhoon season in Hong Kong, but hill fires and cold fronts can still affect the race.
- **Semi-self-sufficiency.** Mandatory kit must be carried at all times and can be checked at random; a missing item can lead to a time penalty or disqualification. Pacers and accompanying runners are strictly prohibited, and outside support is allowed only at checkpoints.

## Getting there

The start and finish require completely different plans. All of the following comes from the official 2027 transport page.

**To the start (Pak Tam Chung, Tai Mong Tsai Road)**

- **Taxi.** From Hong Kong Island, under an hour and a little under HK$300. From Kowloon, about 45 minutes and about HK$200. Showing the driver the Chinese address 西貢大網仔路北潭涌 is the most reliable approach.
- **Public transport.** Bus or minibus routes to the start involve a change in Sai Kung Town. From Kowloon: bus 92 from Diamond Hill MTR bus terminus, from 6 am every 30 minutes; minibus 1A from Choi Hung MTR station, from 6.30 am, leaving when full. From Hong Kong Island: MTR to Hang Hau, then minibus 101M from 5.30 am, leaving when full; or MTR to Choi Hung, then minibus 1A.
- **Sai Kung Town to Pak Tam Chung.** Bus 94 from 6 am every 30 minutes; minibus 9 from 6.15 am every 30 minutes.
- **Official travel service.** GL Tours of Hong Kong is appointed as the official travel services partner for the 2027 Hong Kong 100, providing hotel accommodation, transfers and other travel services. The organiser recommends using it to reduce traffic congestion on the way to the start.

**Leaving the finish (Rotary Club Park, Tai Mo Shan Road)**

- **Free shuttle.** Between the finish and Tsuen Wan MTR station, operating from 5.30 pm on 23 January 2027 to 2.30 pm on 24 January 2027, leaving when full. A later passage on the same page says the service runs from 5.30 pm Saturday to 11 am Sunday; the two end times disagree, so the official page should be rechecked before relying on it.
- **Road closure.** Tai Mo Shan Road to the finish is closed from 08:00 on 23 January to 17:00 on 24 January 2027. No participant cars or taxis may enter or stay on Tai Mo Shan Road or at the finish (Rotary Club car park) during that period.
- **Bus 51.** Stops at the junction of Route Twisk and Tai Mo Shan Road, about 3 minutes' walk from the finish, and runs to Tsuen Wan Railway Station. It does not run overnight: it stops at around 11 pm and resumes at 6 am, every 30–40 minutes.
- **Spectating and support.** The start at Pak Tam Chung is car accessible with limited parking. CP1 East Dam has a race-day road closure and no vehicle access. CP3 Pak Tam Au, CP4 Pak Sha O and CP6 Kei Ling Ha are reachable by public transport or taxi. CP8 and CP10 are generally reachable only on foot, about an hour. CP9 Shing Mun is car accessible with very limited parking.
- **Self-driving is not recommended.** Parking at the start is limited, there is none at the finish, and Tai Mo Shan Road is closed during the race window. Staying in the city and using public transport or the official shuttle is the practical approach set out by the organiser.

**From the airport**

Hong Kong International Airport (HKG) is the nearest international gateway. The airport's official transport pages list three ways into the city: the Airport Express (described by the airport as the quickest way to and from the city), public buses serving all of Hong Kong, and taxis. There is no direct rail link to Sai Kung. The realistic chain is airport → city (MTR or bus) → Sai Kung Town → bus 94 or minibus 9 → Pak Tam Chung. Early-morning or race-day services can be affected by the event, so the transport operators' information for the day should be checked. The race transport page does not promise an airport shuttle; the only airport-adjacent option it names is GL Tours' paid transfer service.

## Season and weather

The 2026 edition was held on 24 January 2026. To give a reference for training and kit in the same window, this page queried the Open-Meteo historical archive for 17–25 January 2026 (seven days before to one day after the race, nine days in total) at a Hong Kong grid point:

- Grid point: 22.3902°N, 114.2236°E, elevation 380 m, timezone Asia/Hong_Kong.
- Daily maximum: 13.9°C to 23.4°C, median 18.2°C.
- Daily minimum: 6.5°C to 12.8°C, median 10.3°C.
- Precipitation: one day with any rain (0.1 mm on 23 January); no day reached 1 mm.
- Race day, 24 January: maximum 18.0°C, minimum 7.2°C, no precipitation.

These are **grid-point modelled historical values, not measurements on the course**, and they do not represent conditions on Tai Mo Shan. The grid point sits at 380 m while the course reaches 957 m; the summit is exposed and windy, so wind chill in the early morning and at night will be noticeably lower than the urban figures. The Hong Kong Observatory's long-term climate description points the same way: January and February are cloudier, occasional cold fronts bring dry northerly winds, urban temperatures commonly drop below 10°C, and sub-zero temperatures and frost occur at times on high ground and in the New Territories. January and December are usually the driest months. "Dry" does not mean "warm", and it certainly does not mean "not cold on the summit". A cold front can drop temperatures overnight, and wet cold combined with wind is harder to manage than low temperature alone. Kit should be planned for a conservative scenario of about 7°C at the start, wind on the summit and possible rain, not for an urban afternoon at 18°C.

The sample is also limited: nine days from a single year. It answers "what the most recent race week looked like", not "the probability distribution across many Januarys". For climate probabilities, the Hong Kong Observatory's long-term statistics are the appropriate source; this page does not turn a nine-day sample into a climate conclusion.

## Difficulty rating

The organiser does not publish a single difficulty grade, and this site does not invent an official one. The assessment below rests on verifiable dimensions and is an editorial judgement:

- **Distance.** Official 2027 basis 100 km; the 2026 course as run was 92.9 km. The two must not be conflated.
- **Climb.** 5,142 m on the 2027 race card, described as "over 5,000 m" on the course page, with most of it in the second half.
- **Time limit.** 30 hours. That is relatively generous for 100 km, but it also means more of the field will spend a full night, and possibly a second day, on the course.
- **Technicality.** The organiser explicitly describes rocky, uneven and steep ground, plus beaches, stone steps, jungle trail, sealed road and ridge dirt. The surface changes constantly.
- **Altitude.** The high point is only 957 m, so altitude sickness is not a concern; an exposed 957 m ridge in January wind is still not mild.
- **Escapability.** CP8 and CP10 can only be left on foot, CP1 is behind a race-day road closure, and rescue and escape costs rise sharply in the second half.
- **Environment.** Humidity can still be high in January and the organiser repeatedly stresses hydration; monkeys steal food; parts of the course have no mobile signal.

Taken together, this is a high-load, low-altitude, moderately technical 100 km trail race. It does not demand snow skills or altitude acclimatisation, but it places heavy demands on sustained climbing, surface changes, night running and heat-humidity management. Pacing the first half like a road marathon will almost certainly be paid for on Needle Hill and Tai Mo Shan. For pace arithmetic, this site's [pace calculator](/en/tools/pace/) is available; for the difference between marathon distance and 100 km, read [the marathon distance](/en/knowledge/marathon-distance/); for splitting target times by segment, use the [splits tool](/en/tools/splits/). These are site tools, not official pacing advice.

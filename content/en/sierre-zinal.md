## What this is: five 4,000 m peaks, not five climbs

Article 3 of the official rules defines the adult course: Sierre to Zinal, also called the "Race of the Five Four Thousand Meter Peaks".

That is the organiser's wording, not this page's paraphrase.

The history page explains the name: the race was conceived by Jean-Claude Pont, a doctor of mathematics, professor of the history and philosophy of science and a certified Swiss mountain guide.

The same page says the race would be named, not just Sierre-Zinal, but the "Race of the Five 4000 m Peaks".

The five 4,000 m peaks are the Imperial Crown above the valleys the course crosses, not five summits that runners climb.

The official history page names them as the Weisshorn, Zinalrothorn, Obergabelhorn, Matterhorn and Dent Blanche.

That page also prints a height for each; this page uses the names only and not the heights, because no second independent source was checked against them.

Runners traverse the flanks of these mountains; they do not climb the five summits.

That is why this is a 31 km mountain race rather than a vertical collection linking five peaks.

The official course plan says the route crosses forests, alpine pastures and alpine ridges.

The French homepage calls the course "très engageant", very demanding.

That is the organiser's own wording: an editorial description, not a measurement.

In the racing landscape it belongs to both the Golden Trail World Series and the WMRA.

The organiser's broadcast page says Sierre-Zinal is "Intégrée aux circuits internationaux Golden Trail World Series et WMRA".

The Golden Trail Series report calls the 2026 running the fifth stop of that season's series.

So the identity rests on three quotations: the course definition in Article 3, the naming story on the history page, and the series affiliation on the broadcast page.

## The course: 24 km up, 7 km down

The official figures are 31 km, 2,200 m of ascent and 1,100 m of descent.

The course plan splits that into 11 km from Sierre to Chandolin and 20 km from Chandolin to Zinal.

The same page says the route has "more than 2,000 m of positive elevation gain", a more conservative wording than the rules' 2,200 m.

World Athletics' 2026 race report gives a finer profile: the race starts at 560 m in Sierre and climbs continuously over the first 24 km to a high point of 2,424 m.

Then comes a 7 km descent to the finish in Zinal.

World Athletics' 2023 preview puts the finish at 1,680 m.

The 560 m start and the 1,680 m finish come from reports in two different years, so this page lists both numbers without deriving a separate "net drop" from them.

The rhythm is therefore simple: three quarters climbing, one quarter descending.

That is the main difference from long mountain ultras: no dozens of kilometres of rolling transitions and no repeated ups and downs.

The 2,424 m high point comes at 24 km, with only 7 km left to the finish.

In other words, the hardest climbing occupies most of the race, and only at the end does the gradient turn downhill.

Article 7 of the rules says the Runner category must use the route via Niouc, while the Tourist category can choose between Niouc and the Chapelle Saint-Antoine.

So two routes exist on the same day, but only Niouc is the Runner race route.

The rules also allow runners to take shortcuts on the final descent between two points of the marked route.

That permission applies to the final descent only, not to the climb.

The organiser has not said whether the 2027 route changes; figures in brackets in the rules can change from edition to edition and become final when registration opens.

The course plan carries its own disclaimer: information may change, and the race rules, the authorities' orders and on-site signage take precedence.

## Two categories: one course, two races

Sierre-Zinal splits runners into Runner and Tourist, with 4:30 as the dividing line.

Article 5 of the rules says anyone faster than 4:30 is a Runner and anyone slower is a Tourist.

Tourists receive an official time but no ranking, and the race cannot be counted towards ITRA or UTMB indexes.

That is the point of the rule: one course, two purposes.

The Tourist start is at night, in waves between 04:45 and 06:15.

Runner women start at 10:30 and the mixed field between 11:00 and 11:20.

Because of the early start, the rules note that Tourist runners may find a torch or headlamp useful, but the organiser provides no lamp deposit.

The minimum age is 10 for Tourist and 18 for Runner, with no upper age limit in either.

The junior race runs from Chandolin to Zinal for 10 to 17 year-olds.

Article 8 of the rules gives the junior race as 19 km with 800 m of ascent and 1,100 m of descent.

The course plan describes the junior course as 20 km from Chandolin to Zinal.

The two figures differ by 1 km; both appear on official pages and both are kept here.

The history page says the Tourist category is unique to Sierre-Zinal, created so that people who simply want to run together and test themselves have a place.

The same page sums up the philosophy as "the heart before the watch".

That is why this page treats the two categories as two races, not as two paces within one race.

## Kit and rules: there is no mandatory kit list

The official rules put it plainly: "There is no apparel requirement".

The same page notes that part of the course lies above 2,000 m and advises runners to adapt their clothing to conditions.

This is not a checklist of mandatory items; it places the judgement on the runner.

Poles are banned in every category.

Headphones are banned too.

Both are hard rules, not advice.

The cut-offs are absolute clock times, not elapsed limits: Chandolin 10:40:59 for Tourist and 14:10:59 for Runner; Tignousa 12:00:59 and 14:40:59; Hôtel Weisshorn 13:30:59 and 15:20:59; finish 17:30:59.

On refreshments, the rules list seven named points: Beauregard at km 4, Ponchette at km 7.5, Chandolin at km 12, Tignousa at km 16, Hôtel Weisshorn at km 20, Nava at km 24 (water only) and Barneuza at km 27.

The course plan, by contrast, says the route has nine refreshment points.

The organiser does not explain the gap between seven and nine; both are kept here.

One thing needs saying: a 31 km mountain race naturally has refreshments and cut-offs, but they are not the organising logic of this course.

What shapes the race is the 24 km climb, the 2,424 m high point and the final 7 km descent.

Using aid stations and a cut-off table as the spine would turn a 31 km mountain race into a shrunken ultramarathon, which is the wrong frame.

This page covers kit and cut-offs in one section and does not walk through them station by station.

## Records and history

The official records page gives the men's record as 2:25:34 by Kilian Jornet in 2024.

The same page gives the women's record as 2:49:20 by Maude Mathys in 2019.

Those are the figures the official records page currently uses.

World Athletics' 2023 preview says Jornet ran 2:25:35 and Mathys 2:49:20 in 2019.

The 2:25:35 differs from the official 2:25:34 by one second; the records page dates the faster 2:25:34 to 2024.

On the women's side, the same World Athletics preview says Mathys later lowered the best mark to 2:46:03.

The official winners table does record Mathys at 2:46:03 in 2021.

But a footnote on that table says times from the 2020 and 2021 editions are not officially recognised because of course changes and exceptional race conditions.

So the records page adopts 2:49:20 from 2019 and does not adopt 2:46:03.

Two conventions therefore coexist: the time that was run, and the time officially recognised as a record.

This page prints both and does not decide which is the "real" women's record.

The history page says the first edition was held in 1974.

The winners table runs from 1974 to 2025; the 2026 result is taken from World Athletics and the Golden Trail Series report instead.

The history of African participation can be traced through several verifiable points on that table.

The 1996 men's winner was Eticha Tesfaye of Ethiopia in 2:41:05.

The 2003 women's winner was Tsige Worku of Ethiopia in 3:16:07.

The 2017 and 2018 women's winners were Lucy Wambui Murigi of Kenya in 2:58:40 and 2:57:54.

The 2023, 2025 and 2026 men's winners were all Kiriago of Kenya.

The 2024 and 2025 women's winners were Joyline Chepngeno and Caroline Kimutai, both of Kenya.

These are points on the winners table, not a full picture of the field.

The total number of African entrants, their finishing rate and their nationality breakdown are not reliably available to this page and are marked unknown.

Likewise, no official series of finisher numbers was found, so this page makes no claim that elite density is rising year on year.

The organiser's participants section has a dedicated page titled "Participants : accès et transports"; the 2026 edition says every participant must reach the welcome zone at least 45 minutes before their start, and that a free shuttle runs from the bus stop in front of Restaurant Le Bourgeois to the start welcome zone, every 10 minutes from 03:30 to 05:45 for tourists and from 09:30 to 10:45 for runners.

A special Geneva–Sierre night train leaves Geneva at 01:54 and reaches Sierre at 04:10, calling at Nyon, Morges, Renens, Lausanne, Vevey, Montreux, Aigle, St-Maurice, Martigny and Sion; discounted CFF tickets of up to 50% are available in limited quantities.

On regular rail, the IR90 and IR95 each run one train per hour per direction on the Brig–Genève-Aéroport corridor, regional trains run one to two per hour per direction, and up to four Geneva–Sierre trains run per hour during the day; buses back from Zinal to Sierre start at 12:00, and the supporters' bus reaches Zinal no later than 08:50.

On the 2026 race day the Anniviers road from Sierre was interrupted from 04:40 to 05:25, 05:40 to 06:25, 10:25 to 10:35 and 10:55 to 11:30; the Vissoie–Sierre descent was closed from 10:25 to 12:30 with a detour via Vercorin, Zinal–Vissoie was closed from 11:45 to 13:30 with a detour via Grimentz, and Ayer–Chandolin was closed from 06:00 to 14:00 with access via Vissoie.

Parking is limited and the organiser recommends public transport; vans and campervans use Parking Cremo – Îles Falcon in Sierre for CHF 5, open from Friday 17:00 to Sunday evening, with no reservation and first-come, first-served access.

All of these timetables, closures and parking arrangements are 2026-edition information; whether they carry over to 7 August 2027 has not been published, so the 2027 official page remains the reference.

The weather section gives regional climate only, not a race-day forecast.

## Difficulty, withdrawal and risk

- **Difficulty:** 31 km, +2,200 / −1,100 m, almost continuous climbing for the first 24 km to 2,424 m, then 7 km down. It is not an ultra, but altitude, steep up-and-down and elite pace make the short distance deceptive. There is no mandatory kit list; the rules only advise dressing for conditions above 2,000 m and leave the judgement to the runner.
- **Cut-offs are absolute clock times:** Chandolin Tourist 10:40:59 / Runner 14:10:59; Tignousa 12:00:59 / 14:40:59; Hôtel Weisshorn 13:30:59 / 15:20:59; finish 17:30:59. Missing an absolute time means withdrawal; do not convert them to elapsed-time allowances.
- **Bail-out points:** Chandolin, Tignousa, Hôtel Weisshorn and Zinal all have road, cable-car or village access. After Chandolin the course is high and exposed; do not cut down alone in bad weather. No unified sweep vehicle time is published, so it remains unknown.
- **Risk:** cold, wind and thunderstorms above 2,400 m; quadriceps load on the final 7 km descent; and descending stability with poles banned. June can be 20 °C or near 0 °C on the ridge; this page does not treat Sierre town weather as 2,424 m weather.

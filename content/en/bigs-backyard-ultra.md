## 60-second summary

Big's Backyard Ultra is the original backyard ultra, a last-one-standing format invented by Gary "Lazarus Lake" Cantrell in Bell Buckle, Tennessee. It is not a trail route with a fixed finish line: every runner still in the race starts a repeating 4.167-mile (6.706 km) loop on the hour, and must be back at the corral within one hour to qualify for the next bell. Missing the return, missing the next start, or choosing to stop is recorded as a DNF. The event continues until a single runner completes one more loop than everyone else; if nobody can run a further loop alone, there is no winner.

It suits runners who already have multiday ultra experience, can hold a repeatable pace over the same short loop, and can refuel and reset inside very short rest windows. It does not suit runners chasing a one-off distance breakthrough, trained only on long continuous efforts, or unprepared for consecutive nights on their feet and very little sleep. Three decisions matter most. First, frame the race as an hourly decision process, not a target distance. Second, practise completing refuelling, gear checks and rest inside the few minutes between loops, and being back at the bell on time. Third, understand the entry route: Big's individual world championship places come through qualifier wins and the At-Large list; there is no public open lottery.

- The core pressure is an elimination verdict every hour: each loop must be completed within 60 minutes and the runner must be in the corral at the bell. Personal aid during a loop, pacing and artificial aids such as trekking poles are prohibited.
- The card distance of 6,706 m is one loop only. Total distance is open ended and accumulates one 6.706 km yard at a time; 24 yards equal 100 miles by design, and anything beyond yard 24 enters genuine multiday territory.
- At the 2025 individual world championship in Bell Buckle, Phil Gore won with 114 yards and Ivo Steyaert was assist on 113; Sarah Perry set a women's world record of 95 yards (press basis, pending official results archive review).

## Common misreadings

- "6.7 km is the full race distance." The card distance is one loop only. The race is open ended; distance grows lap by lap. Treating 6,706 m as the finish distance, or using it to compute a finish cutoff, is wrong.
- "A backyard is just hourly road intervals." The loop mixes dirt trail by day with a road loop after dark; rules ban personal aid and pacing during a yard. It is a multiday endurance and decision event, not an interval session.
- "You can eat your fill at the aid station before the next loop." Personal aid happens only between yards; during a yard only common aid stations may be used, and leaving the course is allowed only for restrooms.
- "The winner gets a fixed finishing time." The winner is whoever completes one more loop than everyone else; everyone else is technically DNF, with results listed by completed yards and converted distance.
- "Night and day use the same line." Day yards follow a wooded dirt trail in the backyard; after dark the race switches to an adjacent flatter road loop and returns to the trail in the morning. Surface, visibility and risk differ between the two.

| Misreading | Rule basis |
|---|---|
| Card distance = race total | Card is one 6.706 km yard; total is open ended, accumulated by yards |
| Crew can hand water or gels during a yard | Personal aid prohibited; common aid stations only |
| Poles are allowed to save effort | Artificial aids, including trekking poles, are prohibited |
| A late return can catch the next bell | Missing the corral at the bell or missing the hour eliminates you; no late starts |
| A stalemate counts as a shared win | If nobody can go one loop alone, there is no winner |

## History and origins

Big's is the original backyard ultra. Third-party compilation records that the event was founded by Gary "Lazarus Lake" Cantrell in Bell Buckle, with the first edition in 2011; Cantrell is also the designer of the Barkley Marathons. The backyard concept has since spread into a global race system. Figures published on the official home page for the past year: 55,000 athletes, events held in 82 countries, 4,396 people running 24 hours or longer, and 708 affiliated races.

When reading the history, keep the event edition separate from the course version. Odd years host the individual world championship in Bell Buckle. Even years host the team world championship: national teams race simultaneously on their home satellite courses, and team scores are the sum of all runners' yards. In 2020, COVID-19 prevented international travel to Tennessee and the event ran in satellite form; the world title was won by Karel Sabbe in Belgium with 76 yards (third-party compilation). This page records the rules and basis of the original Bell Buckle loop and does not count a satellite venue's yards as a Big's home-course result.

At the 2026 World Team Championship the United States won with 1,120 yards, 51 ahead of Australia on 1,069; Belgium were third on 1,058 and ran the last national race to yard 124. At the Belgian satellite in 2024, Merijn Geerts, Ivo Steyaert and Frank Gielen all reached 110 yards (458.33 miles) and finished level; with no runner able to complete a solitary unassisted loop, that station ended without an individual last one standing. Belgium set the team benchmark of 1,147 yards in 2024.

## Course and geometry

**Loop shape and switch.** The course is one fixed loop that departs and returns to the same corral every yard. By day the field runs a wooded dirt trail in the backyard; after dark it moves to a flatter adjacent road loop for safety, returning to the trail loop in the morning. The third-party results database records the venue at 5111 Millersburg Rd, Bell Buckle, with a listed course elevation of 42 m; that figure is a database entry. The official pages publish no loop gain or loss, so this card leaves `elevationGainMeters` empty rather than estimating.

**Geometry basis.** Neither the official rules page nor the results page publishes a downloadable GPX or KML file. The third-party database links a Strava segment as a navigation reference. This card records geometry as `unavailable`: no line is drawn, no KML/GeoJSON is produced, and no third-party track is hosted or mirrored. The map anchor is an editorial overview coordinate for the Bell Buckle area, not a precision start-line fix.

**Direction and repeat structure.** The loop has no point-to-point north-south character: every yard is a closed circuit from the corral, marked around the loop and back to the corral, with refuelling and rest in the remaining time before the next bell. Slower runners must allow passes. Apart from restroom use, runners may not leave the course during a yard, and eliminated runners may not enter the course.

**Geometry compared with other races.** Unlike point-to-point mountain ultras, the geometric pressure here is repetition: the same line is traversed dozens or hundreds of times, and surface, turns, climbs and aid positions must be re-familiarised every yard. Once an official or trusted track is available, the elevation structure can be checked with the [Route elevation tool](/en/tools/route-elevation/); no verifiable track was located this round, so the tool is not asked to fill in numbers.

## Timing and cutoffs

**Hourly start rules.** The official rules state timing and verdict briefly: each loop starts precisely one hour after the last; 3-, 2- and 1-minute warnings must be given; all competitors must start at the bell, with no late starts. A loop must be completed within the hour to count, including the final lap. The rules note that per-loop timing is optional for the organiser to operate, but the return verdict is hard.

**DNF verdict.** Any of the following ends the race: failing to complete the yard within the hour; not being in the corral when the bell rings and failing to start with it; choosing not to continue. Under the rules, every runner except the winner is technically recorded as DNF, and results list each runner's completed yards and converted distance. If no runner completes one more loop than everyone else, there is no winner.

## Format worked examples

**Example A: back in 59 minutes per yard.** Assume each yard takes 59 minutes, leaving 1 minute at the corral for water and gear reset before the bell. At 6.706 km per yard, average pace is about 8.80 min/km (8:48/km), equivalent to 14.16 min/mi (14:10/mi).

| Elapsed | Yards completed | Cumulative distance | Running time | Cumulative rest | Rest share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 24 hours | 24 | 160.9 km (100.0 miles) | 23.6 h | 0.4 h (24 min) | 1.67% |
| 48 hours | 48 | 321.9 km (200.0 miles) | 47.2 h | 0.8 h (48 min) | 1.67% |
| 100 hours | 100 | 670.6 km (416.7 miles) | 98.33 h | 1.67 h (100 min) | 1.67% |

The table shows the backyard time structure: rest is not one continuous sleep but fragments of one minute per yard; over 100 hours the clock is stopped for under two hours. Pace arithmetic can be re-run with other assumptions in the [Splits table](/en/tools/splits/).

**Example B: only a 3-minute margin per yard, ten consecutive yards.** If each yard takes 57 minutes with a 3-minute margin, pace is about 8.50 min/km. Ten consecutive yards span 10 hours elapsed: 9.5 h running (570 min) and 0.5 h rest in total (30 min), a 5.0% rest share. The margin structure is more fragile than Example A: one yard a minute slower drops that yard's margin from 3 to 2 minutes; any yard exceeding 60 minutes eliminates the runner regardless of how many yards preceded it. Ten yards are only 67.1 km and just over 16 hours; the real risk is margin eroding yard by yard with no buffer left.

**Cutoff basis.** Big's has no traditional trail-race station cutoff table; the hard cutoff for every yard is the next bell. The rules page does not publish per-loop timing methods or chip details; that edition follows the on-site timing officials.

## Aid, logistics and crew/pacer support

**Restrictions during a yard.** The official rules are explicit: no personal aid may be received during a loop; common aid stations are allowed. Apart from restroom use, a competitor may not leave the course until the loop is completed; non-competitors, including eliminated runners, are not permitted on the course; pacing is not allowed; artificial aids such as trekking poles are prohibited; slower runners must allow passes.

**Personal support between yards.** After a loop is completed, runners may return to their personal aid area for food, drink, clothing changes and rest; all of that happens only between yards. Affiliated race guides summarise it as: whatever you carry when the yard starts stays with you until the finish; whatever you do not have at the start waits until the yard is done. The Big's rules page does not list crew tents, drop-bag points or spectator boundaries zone by zone, so this page does not invent that layout; the athlete handbook for the edition defines the corral and support-area limits.

**Pacers.** The rules text creates no pacer role: pacing during a yard is banned, eliminated runners cannot enter the course, and there is no arrangement where another runner accompanies you through a loop. This differs completely from 100-mile trail races that open pacer access in the second half.

**Common aid.** Rules permit common aid stations, but the official rules page does not publish food lists, station positions or opening hours; this page does not estimate per-station menus. Historical reporting describes runners eating, drinking and using port-a-potties in the spare time between loops; the actual aid setup follows that edition's field.

**Transport and lodging.** The nearest major airport is Nashville International (BNA). A third-party accommodation platform lists nearby Shelbyville hotels about 74 km from BNA; door-to-door driving time should be confirmed with navigation. Bell Buckle itself has limited lodging, with small B&B and cabin options in town and most chain inns in adjacent Shelbyville; beds fill during championship week and should be booked early. Official shuttle, packet pickup and parking arrangements were not located on the official site this round and are listed under unverified items.

Mid-to-late October in central-south Tennessee can sit around 20 °C by day, with overnight lows near single-digit Celsius; the diurnal swing and rain both need to be planned for over consecutive days. The dirt trail can be warm and sticky by day, while the night road loop feels windier and cooler.

**Environment and risk.** The main multiday risk is not a single extreme weather event but the stacking of sleep loss, accumulated fatigue, foot friction, stomach issues and temperature swings. This page does not diagnose or recommend medication. Persistent vomiting without food intake, unstable gait, confusion, chest pain or noticeable palpitations, or shivering that will not ease should be assessed at the corral or nearest medical point rather than carried into the next yard. ## Training transfer

1. **Train short repeated loops, not one long continuous run.** The output unit of a backyard is a sub-one-hour loop with fragmented recovery; prepare by repeating steady fixed-duration efforts, fast refuelling and pace resets, rather than building only one long run. Pacing principles are in [Race-day pacing](/en/knowledge/race-day-pacing/).
2. **Design fueling around yards, not a 100-mile spread.** Each loop leaves only a short eating window; practise fast carbohydrate and fluid intake. Full-race fueling strategy is in [Long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/).
3. **Expose yourself to sleep loss and accumulated load in training.** The high-yardage threshold is sustainability over consecutive days with little rest; schedule repeated short blocks and post-session-on-fatigue efforts, and learn your own warning signs. The framework is in [Load and overtraining](/en/knowledge/load-and-overtraining/).
4. **Practise night repeats separately.** Night yards run on roads; light, clothing and direction sense while sleepy need dedicated exposure. Night-running notes are in [Night running and traffic](/en/knowledge/night-running-and-traffic/).
5. **Choose gear for a repeatable per-yard loop.** Vest capacity, water system, food carry and layering should close the loop in one yard; pack principles are in [Trail gear](/en/knowledge/trail-gear/). Run through the [Race checklist](/en/tools/race-checklist/) before the start.

## Race-day execution card

**Before the start.** Confirm the corral position and bell times; check the timing/lap device, on-body water and one-yard food, spare light, layers and anti-chafe items; agree the between-yard aid position and handover boundaries with your crew; set up the personal support area within the rules.

**First 24 hours target (24 yards / 100 miles).** Start off conservatively and hold a sustainable yard pace; each return to the corral starts with water and carbs, then foot and friction checks, leaving margin to reach the bell on time. Treat the first night as the first real barrier: confirm the light works and keep the night pace out of the barely-finishing edge.

**Beyond yard 24.** Twenty-four yards only reach the 100-mile mark; most eliminations happen later. Assess margin yard by yard: is the return time shrinking, is food going down, is gait deforming? When margin narrows for several yards in a row, decide early rather than forcing another yard you cannot reliably close.

**Withdrawal points and stop rules.** Voluntary withdrawal is recorded DNF with results by completed yards; medical red flags go to on-site assessment before deciding to continue. Do not accept outside aid outside the rules, and do not let eliminated people enter the course to accompany you.

**Gear switch points.** Prepare for heat and sun in daylight; add warm layers and light before dark; use the between-yard personal area to swap dry socks and worn items. All clothing changes happen only between yards.

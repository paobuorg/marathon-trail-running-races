## Race identity: Chaigutangsi Kuocang Trail 2026

Chaigutangsi began in 2016; its name comes from a Taizhou dialect phrase. The 2026 edition was the eleventh and opened a new decade. Start and finish sit in Linhai’s old town, linking the Jiangnan Great Wall, Ziyang Old Street and the native Kuocang forests into a hard loop.

The identity of this race is not a list of landmarks; it is the structure of the course: From Xingshanmen Square via the Jiangnan Great Wall and Ziyang Old Street into the forested mountains of Kuocang, Youxi and Yongfeng towns, returning on a closed loop to Xingshanmen. The 105K runs through the core Kuocang forests; the 50K and 25K use middle and shorter sections.

## Timing and cut-offs

A cut-off is not a single finish-line rule. Most races set segment cut-offs at checkpoints or kilometres; miss one and you are out. Different waves have different times. Write every checkpoint cut-off on the back of your bib or in a phone note, not just the finish time.

On trail, add night, falling temperatures and navigation error to the plan; your head torch and spare warmth must last to the next checkpoint.

## Aid, support and bags

Start and finish share Xingshanmen, with packet collection, bag drop and finish flow in the old town. Linhai railway station is about 20–30 minutes by road; old-town beds are tight during race weekend, so book in the old town or central Linhai early.

Long-race support is usually limited to named checkpoints; private support, pacers and drop bags follow the rules. A shared start and finish does not mean free access to the course. Runners and families should read the support rules first.

Plan fuelling by time, not by kilometre: on trail, eat little and often every 30–45 minutes and refill water and salt at checkpoints; do not overload solid food before a climb. Never test a new gel, powder or shoe on race day.

## Training focus

Build climbing endurance and descending skill separately: one long climb, one technical descent and one night head-torch run each week. Cut big climbs four weeks out but keep short steep stimuli; start shifting your sleep and meal times to the race schedule two weeks out.

Put training in the calendar, not just “run more”. A weekly structure: one long run, one threshold or climb session, one technical or strength session, and easy running around it. Reduce three weeks out, and keep only a short activation in race week.

If the goal is to finish safely, the volume can be lower than a PB plan, but long runs and race-pace contact cannot be skipped.

## Race-day execution

Run checkpoint to checkpoint, not by overall average pace. Hike the climbs, do not race the descents; at a checkpoint, drink, take salt, check torch and warmth before deciding on solid food. At night, check fingers and core temperature every 2–3 hours.

Race-day checklist: bib, chip, shoes, socks, fuel, salt, waterproof/warmth, phone, power bank, cash/transit card, post-race clothes. Trail adds head torch, spare batteries, survival blanket, whistle and mandatory kit.

Write your withdrawal conditions before the start: hypothermia, persistent dizziness, vomiting, sharp joint pain, missing a segment cut-off. If any appears, slow to the medical point instead of bargaining for “one more checkpoint”.

## 16-week training template

Use 16 weeks as one full build, with the last three as a taper. Trail separates climbing from descending; road combines long runs with race pace.

## Race-week timeline

Seven days out, switch training to race time: run long and eat breakfast at the start hour, and fix your bedtime. Five days out, check bib, chip, travel and bed; three days out, lay out the full kit and buy anything missing rather than waiting for packet pickup. Two days out is a 20–30 minute activation run; the day before is pickup, a course check and an early night.

Work backwards from race morning: wake, breakfast, empty, leave, check-in, warm up, start. On trail, eat earlier than on road to give the gut 2.5–3 hours; sip only small amounts in the last 30 minutes. If the start is before 07:00, lay out clothes, bib and breakfast the night before to remove morning decisions.

Do not plan a long drive in the 24 hours after the race; use sleep, fluid and easy walking for 48 hours. Finishing is the start of recovery, not the end of training.

## Pace and effort distribution

On trail, use effort rather than pace: climbs conversational, descents controlled, flats fuelled. Mark each section A (runnable), B (run-hike), C (hike) and reassess at every checkpoint.

| Section | Feel | Action |
|---|---|---|
| First 10% | Easier than expected | Hold back, check kit and hydration |
| Middle | Steady, conversational | Fuel and salt on plan, check gait |
| Last 30% | Clearly hard | Shorter stride, higher cadence, hike if needed |
| Final 5 km | Limit | Execute the plan; no novel surge |

The pace table is not a contract. Weather, climb and surface make the same effort produce different paces; use checkpoint time.

## Risk management and withdrawal

Race-day risk is not the abstract “be careful”; it is four observable signals: persistent dizziness, cold or shivering, vomiting, and a changed gait. If any appears, slow to the nearest medical point instead of bargaining for “one more aid station”. On trail, add getting lost, hypothermia, night vision and no phone signal; write the evacuation route after a withdrawal on the back of your bib.

| Signal | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dizziness or blackout | Low blood pressure, dehydration, low glucose | Stop, sit, drink and eat, call medical |
| Cold or shivering | Hypothermia, fatigue | Add warmth, enter a checkpoint, assess withdrawal |
| Vomiting or no intake | Gut shutdown, over-fuelling | Stop solids, sip fluids, seek medical |
| Changed gait or sharp joint pain | Injury, stress-fracture risk | Stop running; do not “walk it in” |

Withdrawal is not failure; it cuts risk while it is still controllable. Before the race, put the rescue number, hotel address and emergency contact in an offline note. After the race, chest pain, confusion or persistent vomiting means medical care, not a wait in the hotel.

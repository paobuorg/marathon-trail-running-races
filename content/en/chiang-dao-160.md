## What this race is

CHIANG DAO 160 is the flagship race of Week 2 at Chiang Mai Thailand by UTMB. The official page lists it at 10:00 on 4 December 2026, starting at Wat Tham Chiang Dao and finishing at Chiang Mai PAO Park in Chiang Mai city.

![The temple forecourt and cave entrance at Wat Tham Chiang Dao: a low Thai temple building with an ornate gable, two tall decorated figures, a metal fence, palms and a limestone cave mouth; not the 2026 start arch or a race-day scene](/media/routes/chiang-dao-160/chiang-dao-wat-tham-2022.jpg)

The 2026 race starts here at 10:00; the photograph shows the temple and cave setting only, not a start arch, timing mat, aid layout or race-day crowd.*

The official limit is 48 hours, so the finish closes at 10:00 on 6 December.

The race page describes a route that starts at Doi Luang Chiang Dao, climbs to San Pa Kia and Doi Pha Sam Liam for ridge views, passes the Hmong village at Ban Doi Pui and Monthathan Waterfall, and enters PAO Park at the finish.

![Sunset ridge of Doi Luang Chiang Dao seen from Chiang Dao Wildlife Sanctuary: layered forest ridges, sharp limestone peaks on the right and sunbeams through cloud; not a 2026 race photograph](/media/routes/chiang-dao-160/chiang-dao-doi-luang-2021.jpg)

*Fig. 2 — Sunset ridge of Doi Luang Chiang Dao from Chiang Dao Wildlife Sanctuary (27 Dec 2021, 17:46, Nuttasak, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/)). The race page names Doi Luang Chiang Dao, San Pa Kia and Doi Pha Sam Liam in its route description; the photograph shows the mountain setting in the sanctuary, not proof that the course passes this viewpoint or reaches the summit, and not race-day weather.*

The 2026 event runs over two weeks: Week 1 on 27-29 November at the Doi Inthanon National Park headquarters, Week 2 on 3-6 December at Chiang Mai PAO Park.

Week 2 also hosts ELEPHANT 100, day and night HMONG 50 races, day and night SUTHEP 20 races, CHEDI 20 and a warm-up run.

Week 2 is not the same course as the Week 1 PAGODA 50; distances, climb, aid stations and cut-offs cannot be carried across.

The official announcement calls the whole event a UTMB World Series Major and says Running Stones are doubled for every race.

This race is listed with 8 Running Stones, and the Finals Access graphic is labelled 100m-direct-entry-major.

Both are official labels; this page does not interpret the entry rights behind them.

In the UTMB distance bands, 173 km sits in the 100M band, so this is the Major's 100M representative race. The 100K and 50K races are covered on the ELEPHANT 100 and PAGODA 50-DAY pages.

## Distance, climb and the name

The race is called CHIANG DAO 160, while the official distance field says 173 KM and the time chart also ends at 173 km.

The name and the official distance differ by 13 km, and the official page does not explain the gap.

The official GPX measures 173.601 km in three dimensions, 0.601 km off the published figure, and 170.865 km in two dimensions, 2.135 km short.

Elevation tags in the GPX run from 319 m to 1,730 m.

The race page gives no summit elevation for Doi Luang Chiang Dao and no statement about the source or smoothing of the GPX elevation, so this page does not claim a summit height.

On climb, the race page says 9,800 M+, the time chart sums to 9,840 m, the GPX yields 10,227 m of raw positive change and 9,853 m with a 3 m threshold. Subtracting consecutive GPX points gives a raw cumulative descent of −10,338 m; the highest point in the file is 1,730 m and the lowest is 319 m. Descent is a measured value, not an official published figure.

All four figures sit between 9,800 and 10,227 m; the card uses the official 9,800 m.

Expressed as time from the start: A3 at 9 hours, A5 at 13, A7 at 18, A10 at 26.5, A11 at 30.5, A13 at 37.5, A16 at 45.5 and the finish at 48 hours.

The first half is generous: 9 hours to 33 km at A3 and 13 hours to 51 km at A5.

The second half tightens: the 10 km from A10 to A11 gets 4 hours, and the 32 km from A13 to A16 gets 8 hours.

The last 11 km from A16 to the finish get 2.5 hours, which works out at 4.4 km/h; that pace is arithmetic and is not stated by the organiser.

The chart also prints Fastest and Slowest reference times for every row. At A16 the Slowest time is 07:28 and the cut-off is 07:30, a margin of 2 minutes.

That 2-minute gap means a runner following the organiser's own slow model arrives almost exactly at the barrier.

The regulations state that runners must leave a checkpoint or aid station before the cut-off, regardless of how early or late they arrive.

They also state that revised cut-offs are published by SMS, short message or Facebook in special circumstances.

## Aid, support points and mid-race baggage

Week 2 aid stations run from A1 to A16, and A9 to A16 are shared with ELEPHANT 100.

The official FAQ lists six support points for CHIANG DAO 160: A2, A5, A7, A9, A11 and A13. Support crews need a Support Pass to enter the support area.

The same FAQ states that mid-race baggage transfer is available only for CHIANG DAO 160 and ELEPHANT 100.

For this race, a Gold Bag is collected at A5 and a Red Bag at A11.

The bags are issued at bib collection, only official event bags are accepted, trekking poles may not be placed inside them, and runners may not put poles into them during the race.

The organiser publishes no capacity limit for the bags and no detailed list of what each aid station serves.

The regulations state that aid stations provide still water and sports or electrolyte drinks for refilling bottles and bladders, and that runners must leave each station with enough water and food for the next one.

## Mandatory kit: one table for 160 and 100

The mandatory gear page lists equipment by race, and CHIANG DAO 160 and ELEPHANT 100 share one 14-item table that is separate from the PAGODA 50 table.

The 14 items are a running pack, a mobile phone, a personal collapsible cup of at least 150 ml, at least 1 litre of water, one working headlamp with spare cells, a survival blanket of at least 1.40 m by 2 m, food, a waterproof jacket with hood and DWR protection, an LED or flashing light or a reflective vest or belt, a first-aid kit, a whistle, a cap, bandana or buff, a reusable bowl with a spoon or spork, and a passport or ID card.

The clearest difference from PAGODA 50 is the shell: PAGODA 50 requires a windproof jacket, while this table requires a waterproof one.

Bottles or flasks with lids are not accepted in place of the collapsible cup, and water may not be carried in plastic bags or ziplock bags.

Food is listed with a recommendation of 800 kcal, for example 2 gels and 2 bars; the FAQ states explicitly that the figure is a recommendation, not a requirement.

The first-aid kit must include wound care and pain relievers, and NSAIDs are prohibited.

The phone must stay on for the whole race with airplane mode forbidden, the LiveTrail app must be installed and running, and an external battery is highly recommended.

The recommended list adds trekking poles, a GPS watch, sunglasses, arm sleeves, warm gloves, spare warm clothing, long trousers or calf socks, Vaseline or anti-heat cream and THB 500 in cash.

The organiser may add items according to the weather forecast, with the final list confirmed one or two days before the race.

## Inconsistencies in the official text

First, headlamps. The gear table says one working headlamp, while the FAQ says CHIANG DAO 160 and ELEPHANT 100 runners must carry two working headlamps and two sets of spare batteries, one set per lamp.

Both are on the same official site. This page lists both and does not pick one; prepare to the stricter version.

Second, the app name. The gear table says LiveTrail and the runner-facing FAQ says LiveRun.

Third, identification. The gear table accepts a photocopy, while the FAQ says a photo of an ID card or passport on a phone is not accepted because rescue teams cannot access phone data.

Fourth, the medical centre. The regulations place it at the Betong Mongkolrit Tunnel start and finish; Betong is not in Chiang Mai, and this race starts at Wat Tham Chiang Dao and finishes at PAO Park.

This page records the template leftover as written and does not infer the real medical location from it.

## Medical rules and safety

The regulations state that doctors, nurses, first responders and race control may remove any runner deemed unable to continue, may require the use of any mandatory item, may evacuate a runner in danger and may transfer a runner to a suitable medical facility.

They state that a runner who needs intravenous fluids must be withdrawn from the race.

They state that runners must stay on the marked route, including while sleeping, and strongly recommend the event sleep bib for a short sleep on course.

Some aid stations have rest areas with camp beds and blankets; the organiser does not say which stations, or how many beds.

The regulations list four ways to call for help: reach a medical point, call race control, ask another runner to call, or use the SOS function in the LiveRun app.

The number of medical points, ambulance response time and transfer time are not published; all three are unknown on this page.

On withdrawal, the regulations require runners to withdraw at a checkpoint, or to reach the nearest checkpoint if they are between stations. The organiser does not publish transport capacity at each point, so "you can drop anywhere" is not supported for this 173 km race.

## Race-week weather: what is and is not published

The race runs from 4 to 6 December. The organiser publishes no temperature, rainfall, humidity or monsoon statistics for those dates and no weather record from past editions.

This page therefore does not present "cool season" or "dry season" as a race fact.

### Regional climate (not race data)

The Japan Meteorological Agency ClimatView station for Chiang Mai (station 48327, 18.77°N / 98.97°E, 313 m) gives 1991-2020 normals of 22.5°C mean temperature and 10.0 mm of precipitation for December, and 22.3°C and 11.3 mm for January.

November at the same station is 24.7°C and 29.8 mm, warmer and wetter than December.

The station sits at 313 m in Chiang Mai city. The start of this race is around 710 m in the GPX, and the highest GPX point is 1,730 m, so the station and the course are not at the same altitude.

The December city figures therefore cannot stand in for ridge or night temperatures, and this page does not apply a lapse rate.

The only direction the regional data supports is that early December in the city is cooler and drier than November; it cannot be read as "conditions during the race are usually...".

What the organiser does support is different: the regulations require runners to handle night, wind, cold, fog, rain or snow alone across altitude changes.

The gear page makes the point concrete by requiring a waterproof jacket for this race while PAGODA 50 requires only a windproof one.

No temperature range, rain probability or feels-like conversion is published.

## Travel and accommodation: the Week 2 gap

The start is at Wat Tham Chiang Dao and the finish is at Chiang Mai PAO Park, 62.6 km apart in a straight line between the two GPX end points.

The official travel page and FAQ describe the Week 1 venue at the Doi Inthanon National Park headquarters, including shops, restaurants, camping and gear rental within 500 m and lodging in Chom Thong about 30 km away.

None of that applies to Week 2, and this page does not move it to PAO Park.

The FAQ offers an airport shuttle from Chiang Mai International Airport and Chiang Mai city at THB 400.

The same entry still says the call centre is available from 7 December 2022 and links to the old inthanon.utmb.world domain.

The organiser publishes no runner shuttle between the Week 2 start and finish, no bag truck timetable, no parking plan and no return transport; all are unknown here.

What can be confirmed is that the finish is in Chiang Mai city, closer to the airport and city lodging than the Week 1 headquarters.

## Entry and fees

The early-bird entry fee for the 2026 CHIANG DAO 160 is THB 8,500.

The organiser states that this is an early-bird price and that it rises automatically once a set number of entries is reached.

The race page gives only the 8 Running Stones and 100m-direct-entry-major labels and no UTMB Index threshold, so this page does not state an Index requirement.

Age groups on the regulations page run from 16-17 to 85 and over, split by gender.

Whether CHIANG DAO 160 accepts the 16-17 group is not stated separately, and this page does not infer it.

The regulations allow a personal assistant only at specific aid stations in the 100M, 100K and 50K races, carrying one bag of no more than 30 litres.

This race is in the 100M band and can fall under that rule, but the organiser does not list which stations allow an assistant.

## Difficulty and risk

This is an editorial judgement, not an official rating.

First, the distance-to-climb ratio: 173 km with 9,800 m averages about 57 m per kilometre.

That is gentler than an alpine-style course but the absolute climb is close to 10,000 m, which makes it an endurance race rather than a steep one.

Second, the altitude range: the GPX runs from 319 m to 1,730 m, over 1,400 m of vertical span.

The three biggest climbs on the chart are A5 to A6 at 1,190 m, A2 to A3 at 1,030 m and A14 to A15 at 870 m.

A2 to A3 climbs from 750 m to 1,560 m in 10 km, 810 m net and 1,030 m cumulative, the first real filter of the race.

Third, the cut-off structure: 9 hours for the first 33 km and only 2.5 hours for the last 11 km.

On the official slow model, A16 is 2 minutes inside the barrier, so there is almost no buffer late in the race.

Fourth, night and aid: on the official Slowest times, A6 to A9 falls between 01:39 and 09:19 the next morning, a stretch that runs entirely through the night into dawn.

The organiser does not publish which stations serve hot food, hot water or rest beds, only that some have rest areas.

Fifth, withdrawal: runners must withdraw at a checkpoint, and the organiser does not publish vehicle capacity at each point.

## What this is: Chamonix's 42 km

The official site calls the 42 km "the mythical race"; that is event marketing, not a course definition.

The official facts that can be pinned down are the organiser and the date: the event is organised by the Club des Sports de Chamonix, and the 2027 weekend runs from 24 to 27 June.

The same page says that under the name Mont-Blanc Marathon there are eight races, open to participants aged 7 and over.

The 42 km is on Sunday 27 June, with the women's elite starting at 06:45, the men's elite at 07:15 and non-elite runners following.

The history page says the event began as the Cross du Mont-Blanc on 1 July 1979, initiated by the then Club Alpin Francais de Chamonix, with 450 participants finishing at Planpraz.

In 2003 the event became the Mont-Blanc Marathon, adding a 42 km to the existing 23 km.

Those two dates are official sentences, not this page's guess at a founding year.

As for which edition 2027 is, the official pages give no number; this page writes unknown rather than counting forward from 1979.

On the racing landscape, the history page is specific: the 42 km joined the Golden Trail World Series when Salomon created it in 2018 and was part of it until 2024.

That sentence means the post-2024 affiliation needs separate evidence; the 2024 identity cannot simply be carried forward.

This page found no statement on the 2027 42 km page, homepage or history page that the 2027 race belongs to the GTWS.

So this page does not label 2027 as a Golden Trail World Series stop.

No official statement placing the 42 km in the WMRA was found either, so that is unknown.

## How it relates to UTMB

This is the easiest place for readers to conflate two events, so only each event's own organiser sentence is used.

The Mont-Blanc Marathon site says the event is organised by the Club des Sports de Chamonix.

The HOKA UTMB Mont-Blanc regulation says UTMB Group organises that event in collaboration with the Trailers du Mont Blanc association and the supporting municipalities.

The two sentences point to two different organising bodies; that is the relationship this page can verify.

Both events take place in Chamonix, which is not in dispute; but this page found no official text supporting "shared start and finish", "one registration system" or "the same organisation".

Those points are therefore unknown here, not inferred from sharing a town.

The only confirmed crossover is sporting: the 42 km page advises runners with a UTMB performance index below 350 that they have very little chance of finishing this race.

That is the event's advice on the level required, not an organisational link.

## The course: a fast opening, then the Posettes

The official description has two parts: a fast, rolling opening, then the steep and technical slopes of the Aiguillette des Posettes.

The official page calls the Posettes the main difficulty of the course.

Then comes the descent and finish facing the Mont-Blanc massif.

This is not a vertical race that climbs from start to finish, and not an ultra built on long flat transitions.

At 42 km with 2,540 m of climbing, the average is about 60 m of ascent per kilometre; this page obtains that by dividing the two official figures.

But an average hides the structure: the real climbing is concentrated on the Posettes, with runnable ground at the start and the end.

The aid-station positions show the rhythm too: Tour at km 13.5, Vallorcine at km 23.5, La Flegere at km 34.2 and the finish at km 42.

The first 13.5 km contain only the first full aid station, which suggests a runnable first half.

Between km 23.5 and km 34.2 there are 10.7 km, containing the main climb and descent of the Posettes.

The organiser publishes neither the course's high point nor the start altitude; this page does not fill those gaps.

Nor does the organiser say whether the 2027 route changes.

## Distance and climb: three sets of figures

The first set is the event's own: 42 km and 2,540 m D+/-.

The "D+/-" notation matters: the organiser uses one 2,540 m figure for both ascent and descent rather than giving two numbers.

The second set is UTMB's race database: 42.2 km and 2,515 m of ascent in 2013; 42.5 km and 2,780 m in 2017; 42.7 km and 2,780 m in 2018.

The same race recorded at three distances in different years shows that measurement conventions move with the course and the device.

The third set is the organiser's own warning: GPX tracks produce different distance and elevation results depending on the system and settings.

These three sets cannot be averaged into one "true" figure.

This page uses the official 42 km and 2,540 m because that is what current entry and rules use.

It also prints UTMB's three year-by-year figures so readers can see that "42 km" is not the only measurement.

The organiser provides no GPX file, only PDF maps, a PDF profile and estimated passage times, so this page could not re-check a GPX file header. The verification section links the 2025 map and profile PDFs directly; they show that year's structure and named elevations, but they are not the settled 2027 map and do not amount to cumulative ascent.

That is different from "GPX exists but the licence is unclear": here the organiser publishes no track file at all.

## Withdrawal and risk

- **Difficulty (editorial judgement, not an official grade):** 42 km and 2,540 m of climb; short does not mean easy. The technical Posettes descent, the long descent before La Flégère and June alpine weather together set the real load.
- **Bail-out points:** Tour (13.5 km), Vallorcine (23.5 km), La Flégère (34.2 km) and Chamonix finish. Tour and Vallorcine have road/rail access; La Flégère has a cable car, but its race-day operating times are not published, so they remain unknown.
- **Risk:** the steep technical Posettes, June alpine wind and rain even on a 42 km race, and quadriceps load from 2,540 m of climbing and descending. No per-segment technical grade is published; this page does not call it easy because it is short.
- **Withdrawal:** report to the aid-station captain and hand in bib/chip; after La Flégère do not cut down along the cable-car line. No unified sweep vehicle time is published, so it remains unknown; the 2027 arrangement is not published, and this page does not treat 2026 as 2027 rules.

## Rules and kit: semi-autonomous, not an ultra with drop bags

The official definition is a "semi-autonomous trail".

The field is capped at 2,300 runners, with 6 starting waves and a 10-hour maximum.

There are 3 full feed stations plus 1 finish feed station, a structure far removed from a long ultra.

Seven items are mandatory: a working mobile phone, at least 1 litre of water, a waterproof jacket with a hood, a 140 x 200 cm survival blanket, a cup, a whistle and cash.

Recommended gear is a beanie, tights, gloves, a roll of straps, and a bowl and spoon.

There is no headlamp and no spare battery on the list because the 42 km runs in daylight.

The cut-offs are calculated from the last wave's start: Tour at km 13.5 plus 2:45, Vallorcine at km 23.5 plus 5:30, and La Flegere at km 34.2 plus 8:30.

The finish cut-off is each wave's own start plus 10:00, not one fixed clock time.

That means later waves have a later absolute finish cut-off.

Runners arriving after a cut-off are disqualified and their bib corner is cut; they are no longer treated as participants.

The official page adds that if they keep going on the course, the bib is collected and they are no longer the organiser's responsibility.

This is not the drop-bag and night-section framework of an ultra; it is a mountain race completed in daylight.

## History: 1979 and 2003

On 1 July 1979 the first Cross du Mont-Blanc drew 450 participants and finished at Planpraz.

Over the next 24 years the event grew to almost 1,000 participants a year.

In 2003 it became the Mont-Blanc Marathon, adding the 42 km and keeping the 23 km.

A 10 km was created afterwards, and from 2010 entries for the Cross and the Marathon had to be limited.

The Kilometre Vertical arrived in 2011, an 80 km in 2013 that later became the 90 km, and the Duo Etoile and Young Race Marathon in 2015.

In 2018 Salomon created the Golden Trail World Series and the 42 km joined it, remaining part of it until 2024.

Today the event brings together more than 10,000 runners across 8 races.

The Club des Sports de Chamonix itself has 25 sports sections and 4,000 members and also organises the Kandahar Ski World Cup and the Climbing World Cup.

These are sentences from the official history page; this page adds no early detail that the page does not contain.

## Entry (2027)

Registration for 2027 is scheduled to open in November 2026 in several phases.

On the verification date the official site said only that; the detailed schedule and procedure were still to be published.

The 2027 fee, ballot dates and phase-by-phase quotas are therefore all unknown here.

The 42 km limit of 2,300 runners is the current rule on the race page, not a final figure from a 2027 entry page.

What can be used as reference is the 2026 procedure: Chamonix valley residents arriving on foot, by bike or by public transport had 10% of bibs with no lottery; those arriving by train or bus had 40% with no lottery; other transport modes competed for the remaining 50% through a free lottery.

A runner could enter only one of the four lottery races.

Selected runners had to pay by a set deadline or lose the place.

Entry is firm and non-refundable unless cancellation insurance was bought at registration.

The organiser also requires payment of carbon compensation for travel to and from Chamonix and upload of proof of address at registration.

Those are 2026 procedures and cannot be carried over as 2027 rules.

The 2027 fee and phases will be added when the organiser publishes them.

## Getting there and weather

The organiser's transport page says that during the event, beyond the Chamonix Valley public network, a free plan of buses, shuttles and trains operates.

To reach the 90 km, 42 km and 10 km starts, the organiser lists three boarding areas: Vallorcine, the Saint-Gervais-les-Bains–Le Fayet train station and Les Houches.

Spectators have separate buses, shuttles and trains following the 90 km, 42 km and 23 km courses.

The regular valley network is Chamonix Mobilité's four main lines serving Les Houches, Chamonix, Argentière and Le Tour; the Mont-Blanc Express runs from Saint-Gervais-les-Bains–Le Fayet to Martigny, with Vallorcine as the last French station.

The organiser also sells a three-day Chamonix Valley bus pass for €7, not valid on night lines after 21:30.

The page mentions ATMB motorway car-sharing car parks but publishes no race-day road-closure timetable or parking capacity; both are unknown here.

The weather section gives regional climate only, not a race-day forecast.

Open-Meteo ERA5 reanalysis for the Chamonix town-centre grid cell (45.9231 N, 6.8700 E, 1,035 m) over the 300 June days from 2016 to 2025: mean daily maximum 20.9 °C, mean daily minimum 10.7 °C, mean precipitation 4.7 mm per day, with 57% of June days recording at least 1 mm.

The 42 km course climbs to the Aiguillette des Posettes area, so the town-centre figures cannot stand in for the ridge temperature, wind and snow.

These are regional climate statistics, not organiser data and not measurements or a forecast for 27 June 2027.

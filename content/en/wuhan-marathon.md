## Kit and rules

Road kit should be light and predictable: race shoes, bib, timing chip, gels/salt, a disposable rain jacket and warm throw-away layers. Do not race in new shoes or new socks; keep only post-race essentials in the bag.

The bib must be visible on the chest or stomach, not folded, covered or transferred; the chip goes where the race says. Pacing, bib transfer and unauthorised runners are prohibited; violations lead to disqualification and bans.

More kit is not better. Sort “must carry”, “nice to have” and “can drop” into three piles, and only put the first pile in the race pack.

## Training focus

Make “don’t start too fast” executable: 5–10 seconds per kilometre slower than target for the first 5 km, steady from 10 to 30 km, then decide the last 12 km by feel. Cut volume 20–30% four weeks out and keep one 8–12 km session at target pace.

Put training in the calendar, not just “run more”. A weekly structure: one long run, one threshold or climb session, one technical or strength session, and easy running around it. Reduce three weeks out, and keep only a short activation in race week.

If the goal is to finish safely, the volume can be lower than a PB plan, but long runs and race-pace contact cannot be skipped.

## Race-week timeline

Seven days out, switch training to race time: run long and eat breakfast at the start hour, and fix your bedtime. Five days out, check bib, chip, travel and bed; three days out, lay out the full kit and buy anything missing rather than waiting for packet pickup. Two days out is a 20–30 minute activation run; the day before is pickup, a course check and an early night.

Work backwards from race morning: wake, breakfast, empty, leave, check-in, warm up, start. On road, finish breakfast 2.5–3 hours before the gun and sip only small amounts in the last 30 minutes. If the start is before 07:00, lay out clothes, bib and breakfast the night before to remove morning decisions.

Do not plan a long drive in the 24 hours after the race; use sleep, fluid and easy walking for 48 hours. Finishing is the start of recovery, not the end of training.

## Pace and effort distribution

On road, use three blocks: 5–10 seconds per kilometre slower than target for the first 5 km, steady from 10 to 30 km, then decide the last 12 km by feel.

| Section | Feel | Action |
|---|---|---|
| First 10% | Easier than expected | Hold back, check kit and hydration |
| Middle | Steady, conversational | Fuel and salt on plan, check gait |
| Last 30% | Clearly hard | Shorter stride, higher cadence, hike if needed |
| Final 5 km | Limit | Execute the plan; no novel surge |

The pace table is not a contract. Heat, headwind and bridges make the same pace produce a higher heart rate; use feel and heart-rate zones.

## Risk management and withdrawal

Race-day risk is not the abstract “be careful”; it is four observable signals: persistent dizziness, cold or shivering, vomiting, and a changed gait. If any appears, slow to the nearest medical point instead of bargaining for “one more aid station”. On road, add heat illness, hyponatraemia and cardiac symptoms; do not mask pain with painkillers and keep running.

| Signal | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dizziness or blackout | Low blood pressure, dehydration, low glucose | Stop, sit, drink and eat, call medical |
| Cold or shivering | Hypothermia, fatigue | Add warmth, enter a checkpoint, assess withdrawal |
| Vomiting or no intake | Gut shutdown, over-fuelling | Stop solids, sip fluids, seek medical |
| Changed gait or sharp joint pain | Injury, stress-fracture risk | Stop running; do not “walk it in” |

Withdrawal is not failure; it cuts risk while it is still controllable. Before the race, put the rescue number, hotel address and emergency contact in an offline note. After the race, chest pain, confusion or persistent vomiting means medical care, not a wait in the hotel.
